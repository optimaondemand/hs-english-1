Ten Words

benign

adjective

Kind and gentle.

“Interpreting Hester Prynne’s deportment as an appeal of this nature, society was inclined to show its former victim a more benign countenance than she cared to be favored with, or, perchance, than she deserved.” Ch. XIII

Root Latin benignus, ‘kindly’, from bene (‘well’).

despotic

adjective

Ruling with total power, like a tyrant.

“The public is despotic in its temper; it is capable of denying common justice, when too strenuously demanded as a right · · ·” Ch. XIII

Root Greek despotēs, ‘master’.

speculation

noun

Deep thinking about questions that have no sure answer.

“She assumed a freedom of speculation, then common enough on the other side of the Atlantic · · ·” Ch. XIII

Root Latin speculari, ‘to watch, to observe’, from specula, ‘watchtower’.

talisman

noun

An object believed to have magic power to protect the one who keeps it.

“It may be that it was the talisman of a stern and severe, but yet a guardian spirit, who now forsook her · · ·” Ch. XV

Root Arabic ṭilasm, from Late Greek telesma, from Greek telein, ‘to consecrate’.

vivacity

noun

Lively high spirits.

“There was no other attribute that so much impressed her with a sense of new and untransmitted vigor in Pearl’s nature, as this never-failing vivacity of spirits · · ·” Ch. XVI

Root Latin vivax, ‘lively’, from vivere, ‘to live’.

despondency

noun

Low spirits that come from losing hope.

“He looked haggard and feeble, and betrayed a nerveless despondency in his air · · ·” Ch. XVI

Root Latin despondere, ‘to give up, to lose heart’.

penitence

noun

Real sorrow for having done wrong.

“Of penance, I have had enough! Of penitence, there has been none!” Ch. XVII

Root Latin paenitentia, ‘repentance’.

estranged

adjective

No longer close to someone; made a stranger.

“There was both truth and error in the impression; the child and mother were estranged, but through Hester’s fault, not Pearl’s.” Ch. XIX

Root Latin extraneus, ‘foreign, from outside’, through Old French estrangier.

avowed

adjective

Openly admitted or declared.

“· · · between fleeing as an avowed criminal, and remaining as a hypocrite, conscience might find it hard to strike the balance · · ·” Ch. XVIII

Root Latin advocare, ‘to call upon’, through Old French avouer.

irrevocable

adjective

Impossible to take back or undo.

“· · · the whole richness of her beauty, came back from what men call the irrevocable past · · ·” Ch. XVIII

Root Latin in- (‘not’) + revocare (‘to call back’).

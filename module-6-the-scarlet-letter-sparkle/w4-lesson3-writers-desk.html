Ten Words

obeisance

noun

A bow or other gesture that shows deep respect.

“· · · answered the clergyman, with a grave obeisance, such as the lady’s rank demanded · · ·” Ch. XX

Root Old French obeissance, from obeir, “to obey”.

consternation

noun

A state of great alarm or dismay.

“· · · replied Hester, with a mien of calmness, though in the utmost consternation.” Ch. XXI

Root Latin consternatio, from consternare, “to throw into confusion”.

probity

noun

Complete honesty; uprightness.

“The buccaneer on the wave might relinquish his calling, and become at once, if he chose, a man of probity and piety on land · · ·” Ch. XXI

Root Latin probitas, from probus, “upright, good”.

preternatural

adjective

Far beyond what is normal; extraordinary.

“Far and deep in its own region, busying itself, with preternatural activity, to marshal a procession of stately thoughts that were soon to issue thence · · ·” Ch. XXII

Root Latin praeter naturam, “beyond nature”.

venerable

adjective

Deserving respect because of age, dignity, or character.

“Once more, therefore, the train of venerable and majestic fathers was seen moving through a broad pathway of the people · · ·” Ch. XXIII

Root Latin venerabilis, from venerari, “to honor, revere”.

repugnance

noun

Extreme dislike or disgust.

“· · · it hath cast a lurid gleam of awe and horrible repugnance round about her.” Ch. XXIII

Root Latin repugnare, “to fight against” (re- + pugnare, “to fight”).

repudiate

verb

To reject as untrue or unjust; to refuse to accept.

“It was to teach them, that the holiest among us has but attained so far above his fellows as to discern more clearly the Mercy which looks down, and repudiate more utterly the phantom of human merit, which would look aspiringly upward.” Ch. XXIV

Root Latin repudiare, from repudium, “divorce”.

conjectural

adjective

Based on guesswork rather than proof.

“As regarded its origin, there were various explanations, all of which must necessarily have been conjectural.” Ch. XXIV

Root Latin coniectura, from conicere, “to infer” (literally “to throw together”).

fidelity

noun

Faithfulness to a person, a duty, or a promise.

“we must be allowed to consider this version of Mr. Dimmesdale’s story as only an instance of that stubborn fidelity with which a man’s friends · · ·” Ch. XXIV

Root Latin fidelitas, from fides, “faith”.

recluse

noun

A person who withdraws from the world to live alone.

“But, through the remainder of Hester’s life, there were indications that the recluse of the scarlet letter was the object of love and interest with some inhabitant of another land.” Ch. XXIV

Root Latin reclusus, from recludere, “to shut up”.

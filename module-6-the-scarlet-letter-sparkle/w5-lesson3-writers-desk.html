Focus

A quotation should read as part of your own sentence, and a fair writer grants a point before answering it. How do you write about a whole novel with that control?

Objectives
  • Fit a quotation into the grammar of your own sentence.
  • Punctuate around a quotation: a comma, a colon or no mark, and the period after the chapter.
  • Mark a cut inside a quotation with three periods.
  • Grant a point and answer it in one correctly punctuated sentence.
  • Use ten words from across the novel in context.

Ten Words

stigma

noun

A mark of disgrace; the shame attached to a person or an act.

“But, in the lapse of the toilsome, thoughtful, and self-devoted years that made up Hester’s life, the scarlet letter ceased to be a stigma which attracted the world’s scorn and bitterness, and became a type of something to be sorrowed over, and looked upon with awe, yet with reverence too.” Ch. XXIV

Root Greek stigma, ‘a tattoo mark’, from stizein (‘to prick’).

infamy

noun

Deep public disgrace; a bad name known to all.

“Then, also, the blameless purity of her life during all these years in which she had been set apart to infamy, was reckoned largely in her favor.” Ch. XIII

Root Latin īnfāmia, from īnfāmis (‘infamous’): in- (‘not’) + fāma (‘fame’).

purport

noun

The meaning that words or actions carry.

“The men of rank and dignity, who stood more immediately around the clergyman, were so taken by surprise, and so perplexed as to the purport of what they saw ...” Ch. XXIII

Root Anglo-Norman purporter, ‘to set forth’: pur- (‘forth’, Latin prō-) + porter (‘to carry’, Latin portāre).

epoch

noun

A period of time marked by important events.

“From the earliest epoch of her conscious life, she had entered upon this as her appointed mission.” Ch. XV

Root Greek epokhē, ‘a point in time’, through Medieval Latin epocha.

efficacy

noun

The power to produce the result intended.

“Souls, it is said more souls than one, were brought to the truth by the efficacy of that sermon, and vowed within themselves to cherish a holy gratitude towards Mr. Dimmesdale throughout the long hereafter.” Ch. XII

Root Latin efficācia, from efficāx (‘efficacious’).

eminence

noun

A high place: high rank, or raised ground.

“He stood, at this moment, on the very proudest eminence of superiority, to which the gifts of intellect, rich lore, prevailing eloquence, and a reputation of whitest sanctity, could exalt a clergyman in New England’s earliest days, when the professional character was of itself a lofty pedestal.” Ch. XXIII

Root From eminent, Latin ēminēns, from ēminēre (‘to stand out’).

zenith

noun

The point in the sky straight overhead.

“But he clasped both his hands over his breast, and cast his eyes towards the zenith.” Ch. XII

Root Arabic samt ar-ra’s, ‘the path over the head’, through Medieval Latin and Old French.

sable

adjective

Black, as in a coat of arms or in mourning.

“Here, indeed, in the sable simplicity that generally characterized the Puritanic modes of dress, there might be an infrequent call for the finer productions of her handiwork.” Ch. V

Root Old French sable, ‘sable fur’, from a Slavic word for the animal (Old Russian sobolĭ).

gules

noun

The color red, in coats of arms.

“ON A FIELD, SABLE, THE LETTER A, GULES.” Ch. XXIV

Root Old French goules, a red fur neckpiece, plural of gole (‘throat’), Latin gula.

pathos

noun

A quality that stirs pity or sorrow.

“But, throughout it all, and through the whole discourse, there had been a certain deep, sad undertone of pathos, which could not be interpreted otherwise than as the natural regret of one soon to pass away.” Ch. XXIII

Root Greek pathos, ‘suffering’.

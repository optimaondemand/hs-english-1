Ten Words

caprice

noun

An impulsive change of mind, or a habit of changing one’s mind impulsively.

“Often, nevertheless, more from caprice than necessity, she demanded to be taken up in arms; but was soon as imperious to be set down again · · ·” Ch. VII

Root Italian capriccio, “a sudden fancy”, through French caprice.

emaciated

adjective

Extremely thin, especially from starvation or illness.

“His form grew emaciated; his voice, though still rich and sweet, had a certain melancholy prophecy of decay in it · · ·” Ch. IX

Root Latin emaciare, “to make thin”, from macies, “thinness”.

tremulous

adjective

Marked by trembling, quivering, or shaking.

“· · · with a voice sweet, tremulous, but powerful, insomuch that the hall re-echoed, and the hollow armor rang with it · · ·” Ch. VIII

Root Latin tremulus, from tremere, “to tremble”.

sagacity

noun

The quality of being discerning and sound in judgment; wisdom.

“If the latter possess native sagacity, and a nameless something more · · ·” Ch. IX

Root Latin sagax, “quick to perceive”.

intuition

noun

Knowing or sensing something without reasoning it out.

“· · · Mr. Dimmesdale, whose sensibility of nerve often produced the effect of spiritual intuition, would become vaguely aware that something inimical to his peace had thrust itself into relation with him.” Ch. X

Root Latin intueri, “to look at, consider”.

antipathy

noun

Extreme dislike; aversion.

“· · · a token implicitly to be relied on, of a deeper antipathy in the breast of the latter than he was willing to acknowledge to himself.” Ch. XI

Root Greek antipatheia, from anti- (“against”) + pathos (“feeling”).

remorse

noun

Deep, painful regret for a wrong one has done.

“He had been driven hither by the impulse of that Remorse which dogged him everywhere · · ·” Ch. XII

Root Latin remorsus, from remordere, “to bite again”.

vigil

noun

A time of staying awake at night to watch or pray.

“He kept vigils, likewise, night after night, sometimes in utter darkness; sometimes with a glimmering lamp · · ·” Ch. XI

Root Latin vigilia, “wakefulness”, from vigil, “awake”.

expiation

noun

Making up for a wrong by suffering a penalty or setting it right.

“And thus, while standing on the scaffold, in this vain show of expiation, Mr. Dimmesdale was overcome with a great horror of mind · · ·” Ch. XII

Root Latin expiare, “to make amends”, from ex- + piare, “to appease”.

portent

noun

A sign that something important is about to happen; an omen.

“But did your reverence hear of the portent that was seen last night?” Ch. XII

Root Latin portentum, from portendere, “to foretell”.

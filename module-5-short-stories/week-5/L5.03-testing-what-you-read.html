Lesson
📖 FOCUS
Farquhar's escape was vivid, detailed, and completely convincing. It was also not happening. How do you tell the difference from the inside?
Bierce spends most of a story inside an account that felt airtight and was false. This lesson covers five moves that make a weak argument sound strong, and a four-question check for deciding whether a source deserves trust.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can explain why an argument sounding convincing is not evidence that it is sound.
2
I can name five common faulty moves and spot them in a real sentence.
3
I can run a four-question check on a source before I rely on it.
📋 MATERIALS
▶
Your JSTOR login
▶
About 20 minutes
📚 PART 1 · PLAUSIBLE IS NOT THE SAME AS TRUE

Bierce gives Farquhar's escape everything a true account would have: physical detail, a sequence that follows, sensations you can feel. It holds together. It is still not happening. The story works because a false account, told well, is indistinguishable from a true one while you are inside it.

Arguments do this too. A claim can be organized, confident, and well written, and still rest on a move that does no real work. Below are five of those moves. They are not rare or exotic.

FIVE MOVES THAT SOUND LIKE ARGUMENTS · AND WHAT EACH ONE ACTUALLY DOES
Attacking the person  ad hominem
“You cannot take her argument about the school schedule seriously. She has been late four times this year.”
Her lateness may be true and is still not a reply. The argument was about the schedule; the answer was about her.
Loaded language
“Only a reckless school board would gamble with a scheme like this.”
Swap in cautious, consider, and proposal, and the sentence makes no argument at all. The work was being done by the word choice, not by reasons.
The slippery slope
“If we let students revise one essay, soon nothing will have a deadline and no one will learn anything.”
A chain of steps is asserted, and no step is defended. The first move may be fine; the disaster at the end is doing the persuading.
The straw man
“He wants a later start time, so apparently he thinks students should just sleep all day.”
A weaker, sillier version of his claim is built and then knocked over. The real claim is left standing, untouched.
The red herring
“Why are we arguing about the bell schedule when the parking lot has been a mess all year?”
The parking lot may well be a mess. It is a different subject, introduced to move you off this one.
A useful habit: when a sentence makes you nod fast, slow down and ask what it actually established.
📚 PART 2 · FOUR QUESTIONS TO ASK A SOURCE

Finding a source on JSTOR is a good sign. It is not a verdict. Before you lean on anything, ask it four questions.

CurrencyWhen was it written? For Bierce's life, older scholarship can hold up fine. For anything still being argued about, recent work matters.
RelevanceDoes it address your actual question, or just mention your topic on the way somewhere else? The abstract usually settles this.
AuthorityWho wrote it, and where did it appear? Do not take their word for it. Look them up somewhere else and see what other people say about them.
PurposeIs it informing, arguing, or selling? You may still use an argumentative source. Just know its angle before you borrow its claims.

The bias that is hardest to catch

Most bias is not a false statement. It is an omission: something true that got left out. A source can be accurate in every sentence and still mislead you by never mentioning the evidence that cuts the other way. This is why you read more than one source on anything that matters: the second one shows you the hole in the first.

✎ CHECK YOURSELF · PICK THE BEST ANSWER
1. “She says the ending is a cheat, but she also admitted she read it in one sitting at midnight, so.”
2. “Bierce fans think a story is only good if it tricks you, which would make a card game better than a novel.”
3. An article about Bierce's war stories never mentions that he fought in the Civil War himself. Every sentence in it is true. What is the problem?
📚 WORD STUDY
fallacy: a move in an argument that looks like reasoning but does no real work.  ·  credible: worth believing, on evidence.  ·  bias: a lean in one direction, whether admitted or not.  ·  omission: something true that was left out.
Word origins: fallacy comes from Latin fallere, “to deceive”, the same root that gives us false and fail. Credible comes from Latin credere, “to believe,” which also gives us credit and creed. Bias came through Old French biais, “a slant”; in lawn bowling a biased ball is weighted to curve. Copia: take the sentence “The committee changed the rule,” and rewrite it twice with loaded language: once so the committee sounds heroic, once so it sounds sinister. Change no facts.
⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · FLUENCY IS NOT REASONING
AI tools produce smooth, confident prose, and smooth confident prose can contain every move on this page. Fluent writing is evidence that something was written well. It is not evidence that the reasoning holds or that the facts are real. The same check applies to a chatbot's paragraph as to anything else you read.

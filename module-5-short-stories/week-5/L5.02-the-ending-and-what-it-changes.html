📖 FOCUS
"Peyton Farquhar was dead." One sentence, and every page before it becomes a different story. What do you do with a story that turns out to be a different story?
The most famous twist ending in American short fiction: what the last sentence reveals, what rereading does to Part III, and whether Bierce played fair. A fair twist is planted; a cheat is sprung.

📚 PART 1 · THE REVEAL, AND THE REREAD
💭 WARM-UP
At the moment the rope broke and Farquhar splashed into the stream, what did you believe?

What the last sentence does

The story ends: "Peyton Farquhar was dead; his body, with a broken neck, swung gently from side to side beneath the timbers of the Owl Creek bridge." No explanation follows. None is needed. The sentence tells you that the rope never broke, the river never happened, and the whole of Part III took place between the drop and the end of the fall: a dying mind's last, magnificent invention. The escape you believed in lasted exactly as long as a hanging takes.

Part III reads differently once the end is known. It is not the same text. The swim, the forest, the road home stop being an adventure and become a portrait of longing: everything Farquhar loves, arranged in the order he reaches for it, ending with his wife at the bottom of the steps, "with a smile of ineffable joy." A story that can be read twice and be two different stories has earned both readings. Reread Part III and mark every detail that has changed its meaning. Compare the marks with the ledger below.

THE FAIR-PLAY LEDGER · CLUES BIERCE PLANTED BEFORE THE REVEAL, IN ORDER
1. Part I: his escape plan arrives as thoughts "flashed into the doomed man's brain rather than evolved from it"  →  thought is already moving at dying speed, and the "escape" is announced as an idea before it happens as an event.
2. Part III opens: he "lost consciousness and was as one already dead"  →  the narrator states the truth in the first sentence of the escape. We read past it because we want to.
3. A drowning swimmer sees "the leaves and the veining of each leaf" and hears the gnats and dragon-flies as "audible music"  →  no living eye resolves a leaf's veins from midstream. These are a mind's senses, not a body's.
4. "He observed that it was a gray eye and remembered having read that gray eyes were keenest"  →  down a rifle's sights, across a river? The mind is narrating, not observing, and even quoting its own reading.
5. The forest road is wide, straight, and untraveled, under "great golden stars looking unfamiliar and grouped in strange constellations"  →  the landscape has stopped obeying geography and started obeying dream.
6. His neck is "horribly swollen," "his tongue was swollen with thirst," and "he could no longer feel the roadway beneath his feet!"  →  the hanged man's true body keeps reporting through the dream: rope, thirst, and a walker whose feet touch nothing.
7. The homecoming slides into the present tense: "At the bottom of the steps she stands waiting"  →  the story's grammar itself changes as reality thins. Dreams happen in an eternal now.
Every entry reads two ways: as vivid adventure on the first pass, as evidence on the second. That double life is the fair-play standard. Bierce hides the truth in plain sight and lets your own hope do the hiding.

The fair-play rule

The fair-play rule: a twist may surprise the reader, but it may not invent. Everything the ending reveals must already be on the page, planted where an alert reader could find it and a hopeful reader would look past it. A cheat withholds information the story needed; a fair twist reveals information the story showed and the reader declined to see. Bierce's ending lands so hard precisely because the evidence was always there: he even told us outright, in the first sentence of Part III, and we chose the river instead. Whether that is fair play or something crueler is this week's discussion question, and the ledger supports either side.

📚 PART 2 · WHAT THE STORY IS REALLY ABOUT

Strip away the twist and ask what remains. A man in his last second builds, in perfect detail, the life he is losing. The story's deepest claim is about perception: under enough desire, the mind will manufacture the world it needs, and it will do the work so well that even the reader believes. Farquhar is not the only one fooled at Owl Creek Bridge. The cards below are the spine of this week's quiz and discussion.

✎ YOUR TURN · ANSWER EACH QUESTION, THEN TAP TO COMPARE
CARD 1 · WRITE YOUR ANSWER, THEN FLIP
Why does Bierce end with one flat, factual sentence instead of an explanation of what happened?
The sentence does what the rope does: it snaps. After the lush, swelling prose of the imagined homecoming, the plain style is the return of reality itself. An explanation would soften the drop; the flat fact completes it. Style and event land as one blow.
CARD 2 · WRITE YOUR ANSWER, THEN FLIP
A classmate says the ending is a cheat because the story "lied" about the escape. Using two ledger clues, answer them.
The story never lies; it reports a mind. It even confesses: Farquhar "lost consciousness and was as one already dead" opens Part III, and the body's evidence (the swollen neck, the feet that feel no road) runs right through the dream. A cheat invents at the end. Bierce reveals what he planted; the reader supplied the belief.
CARD 3 · WRITE YOUR ANSWER, THEN FLIP
On a second reading, what kind of story is Part III? Name what it shows about Farquhar that the "escape" reading hides.
A portrait of longing. Everything he loves arrives in the order he reaches for it: his body, the living world, freedom, home, and last and most his wife. The escape reading shows a man's luck; the true reading shows a man's heart, inventing the world he cannot keep. That is why the story is moving and not merely clever.
📚 WORD STUDY
flashback: a scene that steps out of the story's present into earlier events.  ·  twist ending: an ending that reveals the story you were actually reading.  ·  inference: a conclusion a reader builds from planted evidence.
Word origins: inference comes from Latin inferre, "to carry in": the reader carries the evidence in and assembles the truth. Flashback is a young word, borrowed from early photography and film, where a scene literally cut "back" in a flash. Copia: take the sentence "The sergeant stepped aside," and rewrite it twice: once positioned so it ends a paragraph like a dropped floor, and once buried in the middle of a longer sentence. Note what position alone does to its force.

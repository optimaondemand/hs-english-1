📖 FOCUS
In 1909, before television, before the internet, before video calls, E. M. Forster wrote a story about people who live alone in small rooms, see each other only through glowing plates, and have everything delivered at the push of a button. How?
The largest world of the module is a whole civilization in a single story. Forster builds it: one room, one rule at a time, always holding the world below against the world above. The two people who disagree about it are a mother named Vashti and her son Kuno.

Before you read: the story's world

Forster published "The Machine Stops" in 1909, in The Oxford and Cambridge Review. Humanity has moved underground. Each person lives alone in a small room where a vast global system called the Machine supplies everything: food, music, beds, medicine, lectures, and conversation through round glowing plates. The surface of the earth is believed to be uninhabitable. The story opens on Vashti, content in her room, receiving a call from her son Kuno, who lives on the other side of the world and wants something the Machine cannot deliver: his mother, in person.

📚 PART 2 · TWO WORLDS, ONE PAGE: HOW FORSTER BUILDS BY CONTRAST

A writer inventing a whole world cannot explain everything, so Forster builds by juxtaposition: he sets the world below and the world above side by side and lets the contrast do the teaching. The story opens with an invitation: "Imagine, if you can, a small room, hexagonal in shape, like the cell of a bee." One sentence, and you know the shape of a life: small, sealed, and part of a hive. Every rule of the world is planted the same way, through a concrete detail rather than an explanation. Vashti's life is mediated: everything reaches her through the Machine, secondhand. When an air-ship attendant steadies her and Vashti recoils, Forster plants the deepest rule of all: "People never touched one another. The custom had become obsolete, owing to the Machine." Kuno's rebellion is just as concrete. Standing on the surface, he takes the body back as the ruler of reality: "Man is the measure." Compare your two-column list with the diagram below, then reread any detail you missed.

THE STORY'S WORLD IN CROSS-SECTION · THE MACHINE BELOW, THE SURFACE ABOVE
☀️ ABOVE · THE SURFACE (KUNO'S WORLD, FOR ONE STOLEN HOUR)
• Sunlight, mist, hills, ferns; air you must fight to breathe
• Distance measured by the body: "Man is the measure"; a walk is as far as legs can carry you
• Experience is direct: you see the stars themselves, not a lecture about them
• Officially "uninhabitable"; visiting it without permission is rebellion
▲ THE EARTH'S CRUST ▼
🔘 BELOW · THE MACHINE (VASHTI'S WORLD, ALL HER LIFE)
• One person per hexagonal room, "like the cell of a bee"; no windows, constant soft light and humming air
• Buttons for everything: food, music, clothing, medicine, a bed that rises from the floor
• People appear as images in a round glowing plate; thousands of "friends," none ever met
• Touch is obsolete; travel is rare and disliked; the Book of the Machine explains every procedure
• Experience is mediated: everything arrives secondhand, filtered through the system
Notice the craft: Forster never writes an essay about what the Machine has cost. He puts the two halves of this diagram side by side, below and above, Vashti and Kuno, and lets you feel the difference. That is juxtaposition, and the juxtaposition grammar lesson shows how to do it inside a single sentence.

Vashti and Kuno: the argument at the story's heart

The story's plot is small: a son asks his mother to visit, and she does. The conflict is enormous, because each of them speaks for a whole way of being human. Kuno's plea sets the terms: "I want to see you not through the Machine... I want to speak to you not through the wearisome Machine." To Vashti this is nearly meaningless; she is looking at him right now, in the plate. But the narrator quietly takes a side. The Machine's designers, he tells us, ignored something real: the "imponderable bloom" of actual human presence, the something in face-to-face contact that no transmission carries. Vashti has never missed it because she has never had it. Kuno went to the surface to find it, and Part II is his account of what it cost him. In Part II, he also says the sentence the whole story is built to earn, the one the whole story is built to earn: "The Machine stops."

📚 WORD STUDY
mediated: reaching you through something in between, rather than directly.  ·  sacrilege: an act of disrespect toward what a community holds sacred.  ·  atrophy: the wasting away of something unused, a muscle or a capacity.  ·  apparatus: the equipment or machinery built for a particular purpose.  ·  humanity: the human race, and also the quality of being humane.
Word origins: mediated comes from Latin medius, "middle": something stands in the middle, between you and the thing itself. Vashti's whole life is lived in the middle position. Sacrilege comes from Latin sacrilegium, temple-robbing; watch how the story slowly turns the Machine into the temple, so that doubting it becomes the sacrilege. Atrophy comes from Greek a- ("without") + trophe ("nourishment"): what is not fed wastes away, and in this world the unfed thing is the body itself; infants who promise to be too strong are not kept. Apparatus comes from Latin apparare, "to prepare": Part II is named for the Mending Apparatus, the machinery that repairs the Machine. Humanity comes from Latin humanitas, which meant both "humankind" and "kindness": one word carrying the story's whole question, since losing the second meaning may cost you the first. Copia: take the sentence "She spoke to her son," and rewrite it twice: once so the contact is fully mediated (name the devices in between), and once so it is fully direct, down to the smallest physical detail.
⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · A 1909 STORY ABOUT RELYING ON A MACHINE
You are reading a story about people who let a machine answer every question, and you live in a time when AI tools will summarize this story for you in five seconds. Both facts are worth stating plainly. An AI summary can give you the plot of "The Machine Stops"; it cannot give you the experience of reading it, and the difference between a summary and an experience is the exact subject of the story. The citizens of the Machine preferred lectures about the sea to the sea. Read the story itself first; use tools to check details after.
📚 PART 3 · MAKING TWO SOURCES TALK TO EACH OTHER

Forster's story is not only a story. It is an argument about what happens to people who let a system do their thinking. That makes it a useful thing to put beside somebody else's argument and see what happens.

Research writing usually fails in one specific way: quote, quote, quote, and the writer nowhere in sight. That is stacking. What you want is synthesis: making the sources serve a point of yours, and making them respond to each other.

What the difference looks like

StackingForster shows the Machine as a trap. One critic says the story predicts the internet. Another critic says it is about religion. All three sit in a row, touching nothing.
SynthesisForster's citizens worship the Machine before they depend on it. Where one critic reads the story as a prediction about technology, another finds it closer to a story about faith, and the second reading explains something the first cannot: why the citizens keep the Book after the Machine fails.

The difference is the connecting tissue: where X sees…, Y finds…  ·  Y's point sharpens X's  ·  both concede that…. If your two sources never touch, you have a pile, not a paragraph.

Paraphrase, and the line you must not cross

To paraphrase is to put someone's idea fully into your own words and sentence shape. Swapping a few words for synonyms and keeping their structure is not paraphrasing; it is copying with a disguise, and it counts as plagiarism even when you did not mean it that way. Here is the reliable method: read the passage, look away from it, and write what it said. Then check that you got it right.

📝 THE ONE RULE THAT SAVES YOU LATER
A paraphrase still needs a citation. Students often think quotation marks are what trigger the citation. They are not. Borrowing the idea is what triggers it, whether you quote it or not. On a JSTOR article page, use the Cite option, choose MLA, and paste it into your document the moment you decide to use the source. Note the page number of anything you quote. Re-finding a lost article the night before a deadline is a misery you can simply skip.

💪 KEY IDEA

A world is built from rules, and rules are best taught by contrast: the cell beside the hillside, the plate beside the face. Part III shows what Forster has really been building: not a world but its ending, defect by defect, until the humming stops.

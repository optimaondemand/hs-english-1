"The Necklace": Desire and Cost
The Necklace
📖 FOCUS
One perfect evening, purchased with ten years of a life. And the price tag, revealed in the last line, was fiction. What does Mathilde's story say about wanting?
Maupassant's most famous story perfected a technique: situational irony, an ending in which events themselves reverse everything the characters and the reader expected, and the reversal tells the truth. The story is, at bottom, a ledger.

3

📚 PART 1 · FIRST READING
💭 WARM-UP
The story opens on a woman who believes she was born for a finer life than the one she has. Before you meet her, where do you stand on that feeling?

Before you read: the story's world

Paris, the 1880s. Guy de Maupassant published "La Parure" ("The Necklace") in a newspaper in 1884, and it became one of the most imitated short stories ever written. Its world runs on money and rank: a clerk's wife cannot enter the drawing rooms she dreams of, a woman without a dowry marries where she can, and a debt unpaid destroys a family's name. The story opens with the blunt arithmetic of that world: "She was one of those pretty and charming girls who are sometimes, as if by a mistake of destiny, born in a family of clerks." Everything that follows grows from the phrase a mistake of destiny, and the ending will ask you whether destiny made any mistakes at all.

Read it now, twice

1
Open the story and read it straight through, no stopping.
2
Reread and track every number and every price: what things cost, what is borrowed, what is owed, and what is paid in years rather than francs.
📚 PART 2 · THE LEDGER, AND THE IRONY THAT BALANCES IT

Mathilde Loisel "suffered ceaselessly, feeling herself born for all the delicacies and all the luxuries." The invitation to the Minister's ball seems to answer the suffering: a 400-franc gown, a borrowed diamond necklace, and one night in which "she danced with intoxication, with passion, made drunk by pleasure, forgetting all, in the triumph of her beauty." Then the walk home, and the sentence that turns the story: "She had no longer the necklace around her neck!" What follows is the price.

THE LEDGER OF ONE EVENING · WHAT IT GAVE, WHAT IT TOOK · ALL FIGURES FROM THE TEXT
What the evening gaveWhat the evening took
A gown, bought with the 400 francs her husband had saved for a gun A replacement necklace: "It was worth forty thousand francs. They could have it for thirty-six."
One night of triumph: "She danced with intoxication, with passion, made drunk by pleasure" All of Loisel's inheritance ("eighteen thousand francs which his father had left him"), plus loans from moneylenders on ruinous terms
A memory she returns to for the rest of her life Ten years of poverty: a cheap lodging under the roofs, "heavy house-work," money defended "sou by sou," until she becomes "strong and hard and rough"
The last line reprices the whole account: "Oh, my poor Mathilde! Why, my necklace was paste. It was worth at most five hundred francs!"
Thirty-six thousand francs and ten years, paid to replace five hundred francs of paste. Read the ledger twice: once as arithmetic, once as a life.

Situational irony, defined at last

In Week 4 you studied verbal irony: words that mean the opposite of what they say. This week's tool lives in events, not words. Situational irony is a reversal built into what happens: the outcome lands opposite to what the characters and the reader had every reason to expect, and the reversal reveals something true. The Loisels expect that their heroic decade of repayment restores what was lost; the last line reveals there was nothing to restore. Notice what the irony is not: it is not a trick, and nobody lies to Mathilde. Like Bierce's twist last week, the reversal plays fair; the truth was always available, one honest sentence away. What that sentence was, and who owed it to whom, is the question of the character-choices lesson.

📚 WORD STUDY
dowry: money or property a bride's family brings to a marriage; Mathilde has none, which fixes her place in this world.  ·  usurer: a moneylender who charges crushing interest; the Loisels fall into such hands.  ·  garret: a cramped room at the top of a house, under the roof; the address poverty assigns the Loisels.  ·  vexation: agitated distress over a thwarted wish.  ·  elegance: refined beauty of dress, manner, or form; the quality Mathilde believes she was born for.
Word origins: dowry comes from Latin dos, dotis, "gift," through dotare, "to endow." Usurer comes from Latin usura, "the use of money," which is what the lender charges for. Garret comes from Old French garite, "watchtower, shelter": the high room no one else wants. Vexation comes from Latin vexare, "to shake, to agitate." Elegance comes from Latin eligere, "to choose out": elegance is choiceness, the well-chosen thing, which is why Mathilde's eye for it is real even when her means are not. Copia: take the sentence "She suffered ceaselessly, feeling herself born for all the delicacies and all the luxuries," and rewrite it twice: once so the reader pities her, and once so the reader smiles at her. Keep the facts identical; move only the sympathy.
⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI AND THIS STORY
An AI tool can convert 1884 francs into today's money and summarize this plot in three sentences. What it cannot do is weigh ten years of one particular life against one evening, which is the measurement the story performs on you as you read. Do the reading; save the currency conversion for afterward if you are curious.

💪 KEY IDEA

Situational irony is a reversal built into events: the outcome lands opposite to every reasonable expectation, and the reversal tells the truth. Maupassant's last line does not change what happened; it changes what everything that happened was worth.

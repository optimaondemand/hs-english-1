The Power of the Final Sentence
Final sentences
🎯 FOCUS
The last sentence is the last pressure your story ever puts on a reader. What should it carry, and where should the weight land?
Four final sentences from this module go on the board, each making a different move. The rules they share: end on revelation, reversal, or image, never on explanation, and put the weight word last.
📚 PART 1 · WARM-UP
💭 THINK BACK
Four stories, four last lines. Which ending has stayed with you longest since you read it?
📚 PART 2 · THE LESSON

English sentences carry their emphasis at the end: the final position is the stressed position, the place the reader's mind rests. A final sentence, then, is emphasis squared: the stressed position of the whole story. The four endings on the board below use that position four different ways, but notice what none of them does. None explains. None says "and so Mathilde learned that honesty matters." The moment a story explains itself, it stops trusting its reader, and the reader feels the trust withdrawn. Study the board, then take the moves apart in the cards.

ONE RULE TO REMEMBER
End on a revelation, a reversal, or an image; never on an explanation. Put the weight word in the final position, and stop the story the moment the weight lands.
ON THE BOARD · FOUR MASTERS, FOUR LAST LINES
MAUPASSANT · the revelation-reversal:
"Oh, my poor Mathilde! Why, my necklace was paste. It was worth at most five hundred francs!"

BIERCE · the cold fact after the dream:
"Peyton Farquhar was dead; his body, with a broken neck, swung gently from side to side beneath the timbers of the Owl Creek bridge."

POE · the ironic blessing:
"In pace requiescat!"

JACOBS · the image of absence:
"The street lamp flickering opposite shone on a quiet and deserted road."
The gold underline marks each ending's weight, sitting in the final position where English stress falls. Four different moves: a repriced number, a plain fact, a blessing that is not one, and an empty road. Zero explanations.
✎ YOUR TURN · DO EACH TASK, THEN TAP TO COMPARE
CARD 1 · REORDER, THEN FLIP
Rearrange Maupassant's last line so the value comes first: start it with "It was worth at most five hundred francs…" Write the full reordered line, read both aloud, and note what changes.
Reordered, the sting leads and the sentence trails off into "my necklace was paste," which the reader must then walk past. The blow softens. In the original, every word climbs toward the number, and the story stops the instant it lands. Final position is the hammer; do not spend it early.
CARD 2 · DIAGNOSE, THEN FLIP
Here is a broken ending: "And so, in the end, Mathilde realized that her pride had cost her everything, which teaches us to always tell the truth." Name everything wrong with it.
It explains (twice: "realized" and "teaches us"); it summarizes instead of showing; it adds a moral the story already proved; the weight word is "truth" but the sentence buries the reader in throat-clearing before it. Maupassant needed one repriced number. This needs a delete key.
CARD 3 · DRAFT, THEN FLIP
Draft one candidate last line for YOUR story: an image (Jacobs's move) or a revelation (Maupassant's move). Then flip for the checklist.
Test your line: Is the weight word last? Is it an image, fact, or voice rather than an explanation? Is it shorter or plainer than the sentences before it? Could you cut every word after the weight lands? If it fails any test, trade words until it passes.
➡️ NOW WRITE YOUR OWN

Write two candidate final sentences for your own story: one that ends on an image and one that ends on a revelation or reversal. Put the weight word last in both. Week 8 asks for a deliberate choice of ending; these two candidates are where that choice begins.

💪 KEY IDEA

The final position is the loudest place in the sentence, and the final sentence is the loudest place in the story. Spend it on an image, a fact, or a revelation; let the weight land on the last word; then stop.

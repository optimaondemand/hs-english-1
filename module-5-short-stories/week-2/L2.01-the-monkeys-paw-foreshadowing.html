Before you read: the story's world

W.W. Jacobs published "The Monkey's Paw" in 1902, at the height of the British Empire. The paw arrives in a quiet English house from India, carried home by Sergeant-Major Morris after twenty-one years of soldiering abroad. Morris says a holy man put a spell on it "to show that fate ruled people's lives, and that those who interfered with it did so to their sorrow." That sentence is the story's engine, stated plainly before a single wish is made.

📚 PART 2 · FORESHADOWING AND MOOD, THE MACHINERY OF DREAD

Foreshadowing is the planting of details early in a story that anticipate, without revealing, what comes later. Mood is the atmosphere a story builds for its reader: cozy, tense, ominous, mournful. Jacobs is a master of both, and he works them together. The story opens in warmth ("the blinds were drawn and the fire burned brightly") while the night outside is "cold and wet." That contrast is the whole design in miniature: something hostile is outside the warm circle, and the family is about to invite it in. The tracker below lists the key moments.

A READER'S FORESHADOWING TRACKER · REAL MOMENTS FROM PART I–II, IN ORDER
1. "the night was cold and wet" while the parlour glows  →  whatever threatens this family will come from outside, into the warmth.
2. Mr. White makes a "fatal mistake" at chess and tries to hide it  →  he takes reckless risks and cannot see danger until too late. Remember who makes the first wish.
3. Morris says of the first owner: "I don't know what the first two were, but the third was for death."  →  a pattern is announced: three wishes end in a wish against life itself.
4. Morris suddenly throws the paw on the fire; White snatches it off  →  the man who knows the paw best wants it destroyed. White rescues his own doom.
5. "If you must wish," he said, gruffly, "wish for something sensible."  →  a warning given, and only half heard.
6. Herbert grins: "Well, I don't see the money, and I bet I never shall."  →  he is right, in the worst possible way. The money comes only after he cannot see anything.
7. "It twisted in my hand like a snake."  →  at the exact moment of wishing, the paw is compared to a striking animal. The wish itself is the bite.
Notice the craft: not one of these details announces itself. Each reads as ordinary fireside chatter the first time and as a warning the second time. That double life is what foreshadowing means.

📚 WORD STUDY
foreshadowing: planting early details that anticipate later events.  ·  mood: the atmosphere a story creates for its reader.  ·  ominous: signaling that something bad is coming.  ·  credulity: readiness to believe too easily.  ·  presumptuous: overstepping; taking more than one's due.
Word origins: foreshadowing is plain English: a shadow cast before the thing that casts it arrives. Mood descends from Old English mod, "heart, spirit." Ominous comes from Latin omen, a sign of the future. Credulity comes from Latin credere, "to believe" (the root of credit). Presumptuous comes from Latin praesumere, "to take beforehand": grabbing before it is given, which is a fair charge against anyone who wishes on a cursed paw. Copia: take the sentence "The old soldier told his story," and rewrite it twice: once adding a detail that makes it ominous, and once making the listeners' credulity visible.
⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI AND FORESHADOWING
An AI tool can list this story's famous foreshadowing moments, because thousands of essays about them are in its training data. What it cannot do is feel the dread accumulate on a first reading, and that feeling is the evidence to analyze. Read first; look things up after.

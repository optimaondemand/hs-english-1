Consequences and the Third Wish

📖 FOCUS
The scariest thing in this story never appears on the page. How does Jacobs terrify you with things he refuses to show?
The warnings in Part I point to two structures: the story's consequence structure (every wish is granted, and every granting costs more than it gives) and its most sophisticated move, the withheld horror.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can map each wish to its cost and explain how the stakes escalate.

📚 PART 1 · THREE WISHES, THREE PRICES
💭 WARM-UP
Which moment of the story stayed with you after you finished reading?

The three-wish shape is ancient; folk tales used it for centuries before Jacobs. He keeps the shape and sharpens the logic: each wish is granted through the worst available path, and each grant raises the stakes. Trace the ladder below, and notice the third column: at every rung, the decisive horror happens off the page.

THE CONSEQUENCE LADDER · WHAT IS WISHED, WHAT IT COSTS, WHAT JACOBS REFUSES TO SHOW
The wishThe costKept off the page
1. Two hundred pounds, to clear the house. Modest. "Sensible." Herbert dies at the works. The firm sends exactly two hundred pounds "as compensation." The accident itself. We only hear the words "caught in the machinery."
2. "Wish our boy alive again." Grief overrules every warning. Something answers: a knock, then "a perfect fusillade of knocks," ten days after the burial. Whatever stands on the other side of the door. Jacobs never opens it for us.
3. White "frantically breathed his third and last wish." Unspoken. The knocking stops. The door opens on "a quiet and deserted road." The wish's very words. We infer them; the story never says them.
Escalation: money → life and death → the undoing of a wish against death itself. Each rung costs more, and each horror is left for the reader to build.
📚 PART 2 · THE UNSEEN HORROR AND THE ENDING

Why withholding works

A horror described is one horror, fixed on the page. A horror withheld is a different horror for every reader, each of us supplying the worst thing we can imagine. That is why the knocking scene has frightened readers for over a century: Mrs. White tears at the bolt screaming "It's Herbert!", Mr. White begs "For God's sake don't let it in," and the reader stands between them, seeing nothing and imagining everything. Herbert has been dead ten days, and the story has already told us, delicately, that his father could identify him only by his clothes. Jacobs lets that arithmetic happen in the reader's head, not in his prose.

The ending: an image instead of an answer

The story does not end with an explanation. It ends with an image: "The street lamp flickering opposite shone on a quiet and deserted road." Ask what that image does. It answers the plot question (whatever knocked is gone) while refusing to answer the deeper one: was the paw ever magic at all? Perhaps Herbert died by ordinary accident, the compensation was ordinary paperwork, and the knocking was a stranger in the night; or perhaps fate punished three interferences exactly as the fakir promised. The evidence supports both readings, and Jacobs guards that balance to the last sentence. A story that explains itself fully is finished when you close it. This one follows you out of the room.

✎ YOUR TURN · ANSWER EACH QUESTION FIRST, THEN TAP TO COMPARE
CARD 1 · WRITE YOUR ANSWER, THEN FLIP
The second wish is the story's climax, not the first. Why? Use the plot-arc terms from Week 1.
The first wish raises the stakes, but the conflict (the family vs. the temptation to force fate) is not yet decided. The second wish is the point of no return: the Whites knowingly defy every warning, and everything after it is falling action. The third wish is resolution, at terrible cost.
CARD 2 · WRITE YOUR ANSWER, THEN FLIP
What was the third wish? The story never states it. What is your evidence for what it must have been?
Almost certainly to undo the second wish: to return Herbert to rest. Evidence: White makes it in terror as the knocking peaks, the knocking stops instantly, and the road stands empty. Jacobs withholds the words so the reader performs the mercy, and the horror, alone.
CARD 3 · WRITE YOUR ANSWER, THEN FLIP
Name one piece of evidence that the paw is truly magic, and one that everything was coincidence.
Magic: the paw "twisted in my hand like a snake," and the £200 arrives to the penny. Coincidence: no wish is ever granted in front of us; money, death, and knocking all have ordinary explanations. The story is engineered so neither side can win. That standoff is the theme doing its work.
📚 WORD STUDY
consequence: what follows from a choice, welcome or not.  ·  escalation: stakes rising step by step.  ·  ambiguity: a designed openness to more than one meaning.
Word origins: consequence comes from Latin consequi, "to follow closely," which is exactly what the costs in this story do. Ambiguity comes from Latin ambigere, "to drive both ways." Copia: take the sentence "He got what he wished for," and rewrite it twice: once so the consequence is clearly a blessing, and once so the same words carry an ominous consequence.

💪 KEY IDEA

Two lessons from Jacobs: a granted desire should carry a cost the character did not price in, and the reader's imagination out-frightens any description. Show the knock. Withhold the knocker.

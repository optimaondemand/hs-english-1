📖 FOCUS
Two weeks ago, situational irony crushed Mathilde Loisel. Today the very same device makes two people the wisest givers in the story's world. How can one tool do both?

📚 PART 1 · FIRST READING
💭 WARM-UP
Think of the best gift you ever gave. What made it good: what it cost, how well it fit the person, or what giving it cost you?

Before you read: the story's world

O. Henry (the pen name of William Sydney Porter) published "The Gift of the Magi" in 1905, writing for New York newspaper readers who wanted a whole story in one sitting. Della and Jim Dillingham Young rent a shabby eight-dollar flat, and it is the day before Christmas. The story opens on an exact sum: "One dollar and eighty-seven cents. That was all." Most of it was saved a penny at a time by haggling with the grocer and the butcher "until one's cheeks burned with the silent imputation of parsimony." Keep your eye on the household's two treasures: Jim's gold watch and Della's hair.

Read it now, twice

1
Open the story and read it straight through. If you already know the twist, read for the voice instead: this narrator talks to you like a friend leaning across a table.
2
Reread the final page only, from Jim's homecoming to the end, and mark two things: the exact sentence where you understand what Jim did, and every place the narrator speaks directly to you.
📚 PART 2 · THE TWIN IRONIES, AND THE VOICE THAT BLESSES THEM

Remember the definition: situational irony is a gap between what is expected and what happens. This story runs the device twice, in mirror image, and each irony completes the other. Della sells her hair, the treasure that would have made "Her Majesty's jewels" look cheap, to buy a platinum chain for Jim's watch ("Quietness and value—the description applied to both," she thinks: the chain is like the man). Jim, offstage, sells that watch to buy the tortoise-shell combs Della's "heart had simply craved and yearned over" in a Broadway window. Each gift arrives perfectly chosen for a treasure that no longer exists. Trace it on the diagram below.

What survives the crossing: both gifts are useless; both sacrifices are perfect. The narrator's verdict: "Of all who give and receive gifts, such as they are wisest... They are the magi."
Notice the engineering: one sacrifice would be a sad anecdote. Two, crossing, make a pattern, and a pattern feels like meaning. Neither spouse knows what the other is doing, so the reader alone stands where both ironies meet.

Irony that ennobles: the anti-"Necklace"

Set this ending beside Maupassant's. Mathilde sacrifices ten years to replace a necklace that was false; the irony exposes what her longing was worth. Della and Jim sacrifice their treasures for each other, and the gifts are the only things that fail. The device is identical, a gap between expectation and outcome, but the target moves: Maupassant aims his irony at the characters' illusion; O. Henry aims his at the gifts, and lets the givers walk away larger than before. The lesson for your own story is precise: irony is an amplifier, not a verdict. It magnifies whatever you point it at, vanity or love, and pointing it is the writer's choice.

The pointing is done by the narrator's voice. Maupassant's narrator keeps a cool distance. O. Henry's narrator is warm and talkative, breaking in to joke that "life is made up of sobs, sniffles, and smiles, with sniffles predominating," calling the couple "two foolish children," and then, in the last paragraph, reversing the joke and crowning them: "Of all who give gifts these two were the wisest." The facts alone could read as cruel; the voice tells you how to hold them. When Jim says, "Dell, let's put our Christmas presents away and keep 'em a while. They're too nice to use just at present," the understatement holds the whole ending: no wailing, no lecture, just two people who already have what the gifts were for.

📚 WORD STUDY
prudence: careful good judgment about what to do.  ·  parsimony: extreme unwillingness to spend; stinginess.  ·  coveted: longed to possess (especially what belongs to another).  ·  cascade: a small waterfall; anything falling in waves.  ·  wisest: having the most wisdom; the story's last word on its two givers.
Word origins: prudence comes from Latin providere, "to see ahead"; when Della's shopping "intoxication gave way a little to prudence and reason," foresight briefly caught up with love. Parsimony comes from Latin parcere, "to spare": the Youngs' pennies were spared one at a time, at the price of burning cheeks. Coveted comes through Old French from Latin cupiditas, "desire" (cousin to Cupid); the combs are "coveted adornments," longed for "without the least hope of possession." Cascade comes from Italian cascare, "to fall": Della's hair falls "like a cascade of brown waters," a waterfall indoors. Wisest is plain Old English wis, the same root as wisdom: the story saves its simplest word for its final judgment. Copia: take the sentence "She gave him a gift," and rewrite it twice: once so the giver's prudence shows, and once so what was coveted and what was sacrificed are both visible.

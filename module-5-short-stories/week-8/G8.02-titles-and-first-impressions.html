Titles
🎯 FOCUS
A title is the only sentence of your story that every single person reads. What should those few words do?
A story needs a real title, and a strong title is the first planted detail. This lesson covers how the seven masters titled the stories you have read, what each title promises, and how to test a title of your own before it goes at the top of your final draft.
📚 PART 1 · WARM-UP
💭 WARM-UP
Suppose you knew nothing about this module and saw two stories on a shelf: one called "The Machine Stops" and one called "A Story About the Future." Which do you pick up, and why?
📚 PART 2 · THE LESSON

A working title does two jobs at two different times. Before the story is read, it promises: it names an object, an event, or a question and stakes the story's claim on it. After the story is read, it pays off: the same words mean more the second time, because the story has loaded them. That is why a title is the first planted detail, and why it should work twice. The masters' titles almost all point at one of three targets: an object the story orbits (a paw, a necklace, a cask, gifts), an event the story delivers (the Machine stops; an occurrence at a bridge), or a question the story sharpens (the lady, or the tiger?). Notice what none of them do: none name the theme. Jacobs did not call his story "Fate Punishes Interference." The title points at the concrete thing; the meaning stays inside the story where it belongs.

ONE RULE TO REMEMBER
A title must work twice: promise something concrete before the story, and mean something more after it. Point at an object, an event, or a question. Never at the moral.
ON THE BOARD · SEVEN REAL TITLES AND WHAT EACH ONE PROMISES
The Monkey's Paw → an object: strange, bodily, wrong. You are already uneasy.
The Lady, or the Tiger? → a question, comma and all. The title IS the ending.
The Cask of Amontillado → an object: the bait. The title names Fortunato's trap, not his fate.
An Occurrence at Owl Creek Bridge → an event, in flat report language. The understatement of "an occurrence" hides a hanging; the calm is the trap.
The Necklace → an object: one word for the thing ten years are spent on. After reading, the plainness stings.
The Machine Stops → an event: the whole plot in three words, and still suspenseful, because HOW is the story.
The Gift of the Magi → an allusion: the wise men's gifts. Reads as simple before; after, it is the narrator's whole verdict.
Every gold title is concrete: an object, an event, a question, an allusion. Each promises before the story and pays off after it. Not one announces its theme.
✎ YOUR TURN · JUDGE, THEN FLIP TO COMPARE
CARD 1 · JUDGE, THEN FLIP
A classmate's story: a girl secretly sells her prize telescope to pay for her brother's guitar repair, and he has secretly traded his guitar for a star map for her. Working title: "The Importance of Sacrifice." What do you tell them?
The title names the theme, the one move the masters never make; it hands over the meaning before page one. Point it at the concrete instead: the objects (something like "The Star Map" or "Strings") or the event or an allusion, the O. Henry route. Any of those works twice; the moral works zero times.
CARD 2 · JUDGE, THEN FLIP
Test "The Gift of the Magi" against the work-twice rule: what does it promise a first-time reader, and what does it mean after the last paragraph?
Before: a Christmas-season promise, gifts and wise men, warm and a little mysterious (who are the magi here?). After: the last paragraph answers it: "Of all who give and receive gifts, such as they are wisest. They are the magi." The title turns out to be the story's verdict in disguise. That is a title working twice at full power.
CARD 3 · JUDGE, THEN FLIP
Bierce called a hanging "An Occurrence." Rename his story "The Hanging at Owl Creek Bridge" and explain what breaks.
Two things break. The spoiler: "hanging" gives away what the escape-filled middle wants you to forget is happening. And the voice: "an occurrence" is the flat, official language of a report, and that deadpan calm is the story's whole manner. Title words carry tone as well as information; Bierce's understatement is doing craft work.
➡️ NOW TITLE YOUR OWN

Write three candidate titles for your story, one from each target: an object your story orbits, the central event, and a question or allusion. Run each through two tests: the promise test (what does a stranger expect from these words alone?) and the work-twice test (do the words mean more after your last paragraph?). Pick the winner and put it at the top of your final draft.

💪 KEY IDEA

A title is a promise made before the story and kept after it: concrete going in, loaded coming out. Point yours at an object, an event, or a question, never at the moral, and it becomes your first planted detail.

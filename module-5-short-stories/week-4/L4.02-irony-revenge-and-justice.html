📖 FOCUS
Montresor calls it justice. The story lets you check his math.
Back in Module 1, Electra forced a question this course has never put down: when is a wrong truly repaid, and can revenge ever be called just? Montresor answers with total confidence, and even writes out his own definition of a perfect revenge. The lesson tests his deed against his own two rules, weighs the ending's chill, and asks what the story, as opposed to its narrator, says about revenge and justice.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can state Montresor's two rules for perfect revenge and test the story's events against them.
2
I can distinguish revenge from justice and explain which one the story actually shows.
3
I can read the story's final lines closely and weigh what they suggest about the fifty years since.
📚 PART 1 · REVENGE VS. JUSTICE: THE OLD QUESTION RETURNS
💭 WARM-UP
Think back to Electra, or to any revenge story you know. In your gut, what separates justice from revenge?

Montresor writes his own law

Most avengers act in heat. Montresor legislates first. In the opening paragraph he lays down two conditions for a revenge worth the name: "I must not only punish, but punish with impunity. A wrong is unredressed when retribution overtakes its redresser. It is equally unredressed when the avenger fails to make himself felt as such to him who has done the wrong." Read that twice, because it is a legal code in miniature. Rule one: the avenger must never pay a price. Rule two: the wrongdoer must know he is being punished, by whom, and for what; otherwise the wrong stands unredressed. Even his family arms carry the theme: a foot crushing a serpent whose fangs are in its heel, under the motto Nemo me impune lacessit: "no one wounds me with impunity."

The key move: take his rules seriously and audit the deed. Rule two demands that Fortunato feel himself punished by Montresor for the wrong he did. Now search the story for the moment Montresor names the insult to his victim. It never comes. Fortunato goes behind the wall drunk, bewildered, half believing it is "an excellent jest." And rule one demands impunity, freedom from all cost. The deed goes unpunished for fifty years, true. But an old man is still retelling it, still justifying an unnamed insult, still explaining that his sick heart was only the damp. Whether that counts as "no price" is exactly what you must weigh.

MONTRESOR'S OWN TEST · AN AUDIT OF THE PERFECT REVENGE
His rule (his words) What the text shows Your verdict
Rule 1: "punish with impunity": the avenger must pay no price. No court ever finds him: "For the half of a century no mortal has disturbed them." Yet his "heart grew sick" at the wall, and fifty years on he is still confessing the deed to someone, still defending it. Passed, failed, or something between? You decide, from the evidence.
Rule 2: the wrongdoer must feel himself punished, by the avenger, for the wrong. The insult is never named to Fortunato, or to us. Fortunato's last guesses run from "a very good joke" to a scream in the dark to silence. Nothing in the text shows him ever learning why. By his own definition, is the wrong redressed? You decide, from the evidence.
The deepest irony in a story full of ironies may be this: measured by his own law, the perfect revenge has a case to answer.

The last line, fifty years later

The story closes: "Against the new masonry I re-erected the old rampart of bones. For the half of a century no mortal has disturbed them. In pace requiescat!" The Latin is a funeral blessing: "May he rest in peace." Hold the sentence up to the light and it refuses to sit still. Is it a boast, the criminal tipping his hat to fifty years of impunity? Is it one last sneer of verbal irony over the man he entombed? Or is it, from a man confessing on what may be his deathbed to someone who "so well knows the nature" of his soul, something closer to a prayer? Readers have argued all three since 1846, and the argument is still open. What you must not miss is what the ending does not say: no regret is spoken, no punishment falls, and the horror is exactly that the story ends in peace, or in something wearing peace's clothes.

Set Montresor beside Electra and the course's old question sharpens. Electra cried her cause to the sky and claimed Justice as her ally; whatever you decided about her, she laid her case before the gods and before us. Montresor lays nothing before anyone. He is plaintiff, judge, and executioner in a court of one, for a charge he never reads aloud. Revenge borrows the robes of justice; the story lets you watch how badly they fit. And notice what the word retribution hides inside itself: Latin re- + tribuere, "to pay back." Whether anything was truly paid back down in the catacombs is the question Poe leaves bricked up with Fortunato.

🌱 FROM READING TO WRITING · THE ENDING THAT WITHHOLDS COMFORT
Against Stockton's open door, Poe's ending resolves the plot completely and still refuses the reader comfort. The wall is finished; the question is not. "Resolved" and "reassuring" are different things, and the gap between them is where a story's chill lives.
⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI AND MORAL QUESTIONS
An AI tool can summarize the long critical debate over Montresor's final line, and it can define revenge and justice from any dictionary you like. It cannot run the audit for you: deciding whether a deed meets a standard means weighing evidence against a rule and signing your name to the verdict. That signature is yours.

💪 KEY IDEA

Revenge claims the name of justice; the difference shows in the audit. Montresor wrote a two-rule law for perfect revenge, and the story he tells to glorify himself quietly files the evidence against him. That is what a small story can hold: a court the narrator never realizes he is standing in.

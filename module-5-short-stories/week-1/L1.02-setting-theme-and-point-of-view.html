Setting, Theme, and Point of View
Lesson
📖 FOCUS
Same events, same characters: why does a story change completely when a different voice tells it?
Plot and conflict are a story's skeleton. Three more tools give it a body and a voice: setting (where and when), theme (the question the story is asking), and point of view (who is telling it, and how much they can see).
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can explain how setting does more than decorate: it pressures characters and shapes mood.
2
I can state a story's theme as a question or claim, not a one-word topic.
3
I can tell first person, third person limited, and third person omniscient apart, and name what each can and cannot show.
📚 PART 1 · SETTING AND THEME
💭 WARM-UP
Picture the same argument between two friends happening in (a) a crowded cafeteria and (b) an empty parking lot at night. Does the place change the scene?

Setting: the world that presses on the story

Setting is the time and place of a story, plus its social world: the rules, dangers, and expectations that come with that time and place. In a short story, setting is rarely just scenery. It works. A storm outside a warm parlour makes the warmth feel fragile. A small town where everyone knows everyone makes a secret heavier. In "The Monkey's Paw," the weather and the fireside of the first sentence already do emotional work before a single character speaks.

Theme: the question under the events

Theme is the idea a story is working on. A topic is one word: greed. A theme is what the story says or asks about that topic: What does it cost to get exactly what you asked for? A good story does not hand you a moral like a fortune cookie; it raises its question through what characters choose and what those choices cost, then trusts you to weigh the answer. When someone asks "what is this story about," the theme is the honest answer that takes a full sentence.

📚 PART 2 · POINT OF VIEW: WHO HOLDS THE CAMERA?

Point of view (POV) is the position from which a story is told. Think of it as a camera. The writer decides where the camera sits and whose thoughts it can hear, and that single decision controls what you are allowed to know. Below is the same small moment told three ways. Read all three slowly and watch what appears and disappears.

ONE MOMENT, THREE CAMERAS · THE SAME SCENE IN THREE POINTS OF VIEW
FIRST PERSON
Pronoun: I · The camera lives inside one character.
I turned the envelope over twice before I could open it. My hands would not stay still. Across the table, my brother watched me, and I could not read his face at all.
Knows: only what "I" sees, feels, guesses. The brother's mind is locked.
THIRD PERSON LIMITED
Pronouns: she, he, they · The camera rides one character's shoulder.
Maya turned the envelope over twice before she could open it. Her hands would not stay still. Across the table, her brother watched her, unreadable.
Knows: Maya's inner life only. Everyone else is surfaces and guesses.
THIRD PERSON OMNISCIENT
Pronouns: she, he, they · The camera sees every mind.
Maya turned the envelope over twice, dreading what it held. Across the table her brother watched, already certain of the news inside and wondering how long he could keep it from her.
Knows: everything, including secrets the characters keep from each other.
Notice the trade: the wider the camera sees, the less suspense each secret can hold. Writers choose the POV that keeps the right things hidden.
✎ YOUR TURN · NAME THE POV, THEN TAP EACH CARD TO CHECK
CARD 1 · TAP TO FLIP AFTER YOU DECIDE
"The dog knew the storm was coming; the boy did not, and his mother, watching them both from the porch, worried for different reasons."
Third person omniscient. The narrator enters three minds at once: the dog's, the boy's, and the mother's.
CARD 2 · TAP TO FLIP AFTER YOU DECIDE
"I never told anyone what I saw at the lake that summer, and I am not sure I am telling it right even now."
First person. The narrator is a character, "I," and may not be reliable. That doubt is part of the effect.
CARD 3 · TAP TO FLIP AFTER YOU DECIDE
"Sam counted the coins again. Behind him the shopkeeper said something under her breath that he did not catch."
Third person limited. We ride Sam's shoulder: we get his actions and perceptions, but the shopkeeper's words stay out of reach because Sam missed them.
📚 WORD STUDY
setting: the time, place, and social world of a story.  ·  theme: the question or claim a story develops about human experience.  ·  point of view: the position from which a story is told.  ·  omniscient: all-knowing.
Word origins: theme comes from Greek thema, "something set down": the proposition a work sets down to examine. Omniscient joins Latin omnis ("all") and scire ("to know"); the same root gives us science. Copia: take the sentence "The narrator describes the town," and rewrite it twice: once so the narrator is clearly first person, and once so the narrator is clearly omniscient.

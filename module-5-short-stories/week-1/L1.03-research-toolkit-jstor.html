📚 PART 1 · WHAT JSTOR IS, AND HOW TO GET IN
💭 WARM-UP
You need to find out something true for a school assignment. What do you actually do first?

A library, not a search engine

JSTOR is a large digital library of articles written by scholars. The important part is what happens before an article gets in: other scholars in the same field read it first and decide whether the argument holds up. That process is called peer review. It does not make an article correct, but it does mean someone qualified checked it, which is more than most of the internet can say.

Step 1: two or three key terms

A search box is not a person. Typing why are short stories harder to write than novels buries the two words that matter under ten that do not. Strip your question down to its key terms: short story form. If you need two words to stay together, put them in quotation marks so the search treats them as one phrase: “point of view” short fiction.

Step 2: the abstract is your filter

Every scholarly article opens with an abstract: a short paragraph saying what the article argues. Read it before anything else. It is there precisely so you do not spend twenty minutes discovering an article answers a different question than yours. If the abstract does not touch your question, close it and open the next one. That is not giving up; that is how research works.

📚 WORD STUDY
abstract: the short summary at the top of a scholarly article.  ·  peer review: the check other scholars perform before an article is published.  ·  key terms: the few words in your question that carry the meaning.  ·  database: an organized, searchable collection of sources.
Word origins: abstract comes from Latin abstrahere, “to draw away”: the summary is drawn off from the whole. Peer comes from Latin par, “equal”; a peer review is a judgment by your equals, which is also why a jury is a panel of peers. Copia: take the sentence “I found an article,” and rewrite it twice: once so it is clear you only skimmed the abstract, and once so it is clear you read the whole thing.
⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI AND SOURCES
An AI tool can help you turn a question into search terms. It cannot tell you what articles exist. AI tools routinely invent titles, authors, and publication names that sound completely real and do not exist. Only ever cite a source you found and opened on JSTOR yourself.
📓 PRACTICE · ONE SEARCH

Log in and run one search about the short story as a form. Try terms like short story form, “short fiction” structure, or the name of a writer you already like. Then note three things:

▶
The exact search terms you typed
▶
The title and author of one article that looked useful
▶
One sentence, in your own words, saying what its abstract claims

💪 KEY IDEA

Anyone can publish a sentence. Far fewer can publish one that other experts had to approve first. Knowing the difference, and knowing where to go for the second kind, is most of what research is.

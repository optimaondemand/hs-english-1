🎯 FOCUS
How does one small word open a door to a world that does not exist yet?
The word is if. A conditional sentence pairs a condition with a result, and its verb tells the reader whether the world described is possible or purely imagined. That makes it the natural grammar of a story about two doors.
📚 PART 1 · WARM-UP
💭 READ BOTH ALOUD
A. The princess points to the right-hand door.   |   B. If the princess points to the right-hand door, the courtier lives.
Which version puts a possibility in front of the reader instead of a fact?
📚 PART 2 · THE LESSON

A conditional sentence joins a condition to a result: if this, then that. The if-clause is a subordinate clause, so the comma rule you learned in Module 1 still applies: if-clause first, use a comma; if-clause second, usually no comma. What is new is the verb. English uses tense to signal how real the condition is. A real conditional treats the condition as a live possibility and keeps the present tense: If the tiger comes through the door, the courtier dies. An unreal (hypothetical) conditional imagines a condition that is contrary to fact or merely supposed. The if-clause verb steps back into the past tense and the result takes would: If the princess were guided only by love, she would point to the lady's door. To imagine a different past, step back once more: If he had never raised his eyes to the princess, he would never have faced the doors.

One more formal touch: in unreal conditionals, careful writers use were for every subject, even he, she, and I: If she were merciful…, If I were the king… This is the subjunctive mood, and in essays and analysis it is the expected form.

ONE RULE TO REMEMBER
Real possibility → present tense in the if-clause. Imagined or contrary to fact → past tense + would (and were, never was). If-clause first → comma.
ON THE BOARD · THREE CONDITIONALS FROM THE ARENA
If the accused opens the tiger's door, the crowd watches him die. ← REAL: present tense
If the princess were guided only by love, she would point to the lady's door. ← UNREAL: were + would
The courtier would never have faced the doors if he had not loved above his station. ← UNREAL PAST: had + would have
The gold underline marks each if-clause. Notice the comma appears only when the if-clause comes first, and watch the verbs step backward as the sentence moves from possible to imagined.

The conditional is unreal, so the subjunctive is needed: If the princess were more jealous than loving, she would point to the tiger's door. Everything else was already correct, including the comma after the opening if-clause.

Any result with would have + a past participle works, for example: …the courtier would have faced judges instead of doors. The pattern is had in the if-clause, would have in the result.
➡️ NOW WRITE YOUR OWN

Write three conditional sentences about a story idea of your own: one real, one unreal present (use were), and one unreal past. A dilemma is two conditionals aimed at each other: if my protagonist chooses this, it costs that; if she chooses the other, it costs something else.

💪 KEY IDEA

Conditionals are the grammar of possibility: present tense for what could happen, past tense plus would for what you are only imagining. Every dilemma in fiction is built from a matched pair of them.

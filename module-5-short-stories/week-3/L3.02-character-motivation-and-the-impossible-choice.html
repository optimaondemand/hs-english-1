Lesson
📖 FOCUS
When love and jealousy live in the same heart, which one moves the hand?
Motivation is the why behind a character's action, and Stockton builds his princess so that two motives, love and jealousy, pull her hand in opposite directions with equal force. Reading her motives from evidence, and weighing two opposing readings fairly, turns a hunch into an argument.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can explain the princess's two competing motives using specific evidence from the text.
2
I can weigh two opposing readings of the same text and argue for one while conceding the other's best evidence.
3
I can describe what makes a fictional choice a true dilemma, the kind my own story will need.
📚 PART 1 · THE PRINCESS UNDER THE MAGNIFYING GLASS
💭 WARM-UP
Recall a moment when you wanted two things that could not both happen. Which pull won, in the end: the generous one or the protective one?

What the text tells us about her

Motivation is the pressure inside a character that produces action. Writers rarely announce it; they plant it in description, in history, in small habits, and trust you to assemble it. Stockton plants with unusual care. The princess is her father's daughter, "as blooming as his most florid fancies, and with a soul as fervent and imperious as his own." She loves the courtier with "an ardor that had enough of barbarism in it to make it exceedingly warm and strong." Both halves of that description matter: the love is real, and it is barbaric, possessive, hot. When she learns that the lady behind the door is one of the fairest of the court, and that she has imagined the girl casting glances at her lover, Stockton gives us the sentence that powers the whole tiger reading: "with all the intensity of the savage blood transmitted to her through long lines of wholly barbaric ancestors, she hated the woman who blushed and trembled behind that silent door."

And yet the lady reading has its own foundation. The princess has spent "days and nights of anguished deliberation" on this question. She has woken in "wild horror" at the vision of the tiger's fangs. Deliberation is not the behavior of someone whose jealousy answered instantly. Stockton shows you a heart with two motives at full strength, then shows you one small gesture to the right, and closes the door on the why.

📚 PART 2 · WEIGHING TWO READINGS LIKE TWO ARGUMENTS

Two readings of one ending are really two opposing arguments, and you evaluate them the way you would evaluate any pair of arguments: not by asking which you would prefer to be true, but by asking which one the evidence supports more strongly. The scale below shows the weighing you are being asked to do. Nothing on either pan is invented; every weight is a moment from the story.

THE SCALES OF DELIBERATION · TWO MOTIVES, WEIGHED FROM THE TEXT
HER LOVE SAYS: THE LADY
● an ardor "exceedingly warm and strong"
● "wild horror" at the vision of "the cruel fangs of the tiger"
● days and nights spent deliberating, when jealousy alone would have answered fast
● the risk and cost she paid to learn the secret at all
HER JEALOUSY SAYS: THE TIGER
● "the intensity of the savage blood" of "wholly barbaric ancestors"
● she "hated the woman who blushed and trembled behind that silent door"
● "how much oftener had she seen him at the other door!": teeth gnashed, hair torn
● the comfort she takes in a reunion "in the blessed regions of semi-barbaric futurity"
Stockton loads both pans and leaves the beam level. A careless reader tips it by preference. A careful reader tips it by argument.

The move: claim, weigh, concede

When two readings oppose each other, a strong reader makes three moves. Claim: state which reading you find better supported. Weigh: present your side's strongest evidence and say why it outweighs the other side's, not just that it does. Notice, for example, that the jealousy evidence describes what the princess felt over many nights, while the love evidence includes what she did: deliberate, pay, and act. Or argue the reverse: that Stockton saves the hatred sentence for last because last impressions govern. Concede: name the best evidence against you and answer it. A reading that ignores the other pan is not an argument; it is a preference wearing a costume. These three moves are used in this week's discussion.

🌱 FROM READING TO WRITING · WHAT MAKES A CHOICE A DILEMMA
Notice what Stockton needed to make his princess unforgettable: not a hard choice, but an impossible one. A true dilemma has three parts: two options, a real cost on each side, and a protagonist whose own character makes both options live. If one option is obviously right, it is not a dilemma yet; it is a quiz question. Project Feeder 2 builds exactly this for your own protagonist.
⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI AND CHARACTER MOTIVES
An AI tool can list the princess's possible motives, because critics have catalogued them for over a century. It cannot weigh them for you: weighing requires deciding which evidence matters more and saying why.

GRAMMAR 2.04
Participial Phrases
Lesson
🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS
How can you show two actions happening at once without writing two clunky sentences?
You will learn to spot a participial phrase, tell it from a full verb, punctuate it correctly, and use it to open and close your own sentences with the motion the Pearl Poet loved.
📚 PART 1 · WARM-UP
💭 READ BOTH ALOUD · NO WRONG ANSWER
A. Gawain was armed by his squires. He rode from Camelot.   |   B. Armed by his squires, Gawain rode from Camelot.
Which version feels smoother and shows the two actions flowing together?
📚 PART 2 · THE LESSON

A participle is a verb form used as an adjective. Present participles end in -ing (riding, freezing, hunting); past participles usually end in -ed or an irregular form (armed, frozen, wrought). A participial phrase is a participle plus the words that go with it, and the whole phrase describes a noun. In "Armed by his squires, Gawain rode from Camelot," the phrase Armed by his squires describes Gawain. It is not the main verb of the sentence; the main verb is rode. That is the key test: a participial phrase adds a picture, but the sentence still needs its own real verb.

TWO RULES TO REMEMBER
1. A participial phrase at the start of a sentence is followed by a comma.
2. Place the phrase right beside the noun it describes, or it will seem to modify the wrong thing.
ON THE BOARD · SPOT THE PARTICIPIAL PHRASE (FROM FITT II)
Sleeping in his harness on the bare rocks, Gawain endured the sleet.
The lord welcomed his guest, smiling at the winter-worn knight.
Wrought as if of paper, the castle rose among the trees.
The gold underline marks the participial phrase. Each one describes a noun (Gawain, the lord, the castle), and each sentence still has its own main verb (endured, welcomed, rose). A comma appears when the phrase opens the sentence.

➡️ NOW WRITE YOUR OWN (IN YOUR ORGANIZER)

Write four original sentences about Fitt II. In two of them, open with a participial phrase (remember the comma). In the other two, end with a participial phrase. Use at least one of this week's vocabulary words (piety, manifold, mien, surpassing, inviolate) across the four.

💪 THE ONE THING TO CARRY FORWARD

A participial phrase packs a second action into a picture and keeps your sentence moving. Open with one and it earns a comma; place it beside the noun it describes so it points the right way. You will use these to add motion to your virtue catalog this week.

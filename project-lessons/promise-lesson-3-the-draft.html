The Promise and the Story
The Draft
The middle of the project is where the paper takes shape. Today you check your question, write your thesis, and draft your introduction, one body paragraph, and your counterargument.
YOUR PATH TODAY
Check your question→ Write your thesis→ Draft the introduction→ Build a body paragraph→ Answer an objection→ Turn in Feeder 3
You will need:About 50 minutesYour PDF from The SourcesYour two texts open
1
Check your question
The check-in that decides what you draft
●
Halfway through, a researcher checks whether the original question still works. Changing it now costs nothing. Finding out in Week 4 that it never worked costs the paper. Feeder 3 asks four things about your research. Here you settle the one that matters most for drafting: is your question still the one you are answering?
My question now
One question. It should still name or point to both texts.
What changed it, or why it stayed
One or two sentences.
2
A balanced sentence
A shape for your thesis
●
Your thesis is the one sentence that answers your question. It must be a sentence that could only be written by someone holding both texts at once. A balanced sentence is a good shape for it. A balanced sentence has two parts of matching shape, set against each other. Lincoln's famous sentence does it. Look at the two halves.
GETTYSBURG ADDRESS · 1863
"The world will little note nor long remember what we say here, but it can never forget what they did here."
Lincoln, in 1863. Two halves: what we say, and what they did.
●
The first half says words will be forgotten. The second says deeds will not. The halves match in shape and set one thing against another. That is the move.
A THESIS FRAME
Although [the document] promises ___, [the story] shows ___; together they argue that ___.
MODEL (GETTYSBURG AND THE SCARLET LETTER)
Lincoln stands inside the standard he asks the nation to meet, but Hawthorne's town enforces a law it exempts its own leaders from, so a standard means nothing unless its makers accept it too.
QUICK CHECK · NOT GRADED
Which thesis could only be written by someone holding both texts?
3
Write your thesis
One sentence that answers your question
My thesis
One sentence that answers your question. Use the frame if it helps, and break the frame if your idea needs it.
●
Test your thesis on two things. Could a reasonable person disagree? Does it need both of your texts? If you answered no to either, rewrite it.
4
Write your introduction
Both texts, the question, then the answer
●
The introduction runs 150 to 200 words. It names both texts, poses your question, and ends with your thesis. It does not summarize either text.
1
Set the scene
Name the document and the story, and the problem they share
2
Ask the question
State your question as a question
3
Give the answer
End on your thesis, word for word
●
Your thesis:
My introduction
150 to 200 words. Your last sentence is your thesis.
QUICK CHECK · NOT GRADED
What belongs at the end of the introduction?
5
Build one body paragraph
Claim, document, story, source, so what
●
A body paragraph makes one point and uses both texts to make it. Here is the order: a claim, the document, the story, a source if it helps, and then your explanation of what it all shows. Use your first meeting point.
1
Claim
One idea, in your own words
2
The document
A short quotation, built into your sentence
3
The story
A moment, quoted briefly or described exactly
4
A source
One of your four, doing work the texts cannot
5
So what
What it shows, in your own words
●
On quotations: put the quoted words inside a sentence of your own, then explain them. A quotation that stands alone is dropped in. Compare these two.
DROPPED IN
Lincoln wrote about the nation. "dedicated to the proposition that all men are created equal."
BUILT IN
Lincoln calls the nation "dedicated to the proposition that all men are created equal," and then asks whether such a nation can last.
QUICK CHECK · NOT GRADED
What makes the second one better?
●
In MLA style, cite as you go. A page number goes in parentheses after the quotation. For an online text with no page numbers, cite the author or a short title, matching the first word of your Works Cited entry.
My body paragraph
About 150 to 250 words. Check every quotation against the text before you keep it.
●
Now run the cover test. Cover one of your two texts. Does the paragraph still make its point?
6
Write the counterargument
An objection stated fairly, then answered
●
Feeder 3's audit asks you to test the reasoning of an address. The same eye helps with your counterargument. A counterargument is one real objection to your reading. You state it in a form the person who holds it would accept, and then you answer it. The opposite of that is a straw man, where you state the other side weakly so it is easy to knock down. Placing the counterargument late works well. Leaving it out costs you the top band.
1
State it fairly
In words its holder would accept
2
Say why it is strong
What it gets right
3
Answer it
Where it falls short, using your texts or a source
●
Your thesis:
The strongest objection to my reading
Write it so someone who believed it would say yes, that is what I think.
Who would hold this view, and why
One or two sentences. A historian, a character, a reader, or a source you found.
My reply
Answer it with your evidence. Concede what is fair, and say where it falls short.
QUICK CHECK · NOT GRADED
Which opening states the objection fairly?

The Look-Up
Writers look things up. Before an author sets a story in a mining town in 1890, they find out what a mining town in 1890 was actually like. Today you choose one thing to check, search for it with the right key terms, read an abstract, judge whether the source can be trusted, and write your opening with three planted details.

From a question to key terms
Narrow it before you search
●
A search box is not a person. If you type a whole question, the two words that matter get buried under ten that do not. Strip your question down to its key terms, the two or three words that carry the meaning. When two words must stay together, put them in quotation marks so the search treats them as one phrase.
A MODEL
Question: how much did it cost to send a telegram, and why did people write them so short? Key terms: telegram cost per word. A phrase kept together: “per word” telegram.

Find a source and read its abstract
JSTOR, one search, one decision
●
JSTOR is a large digital library of articles written by scholars. Before an article gets in, other scholars in the same field read it and decide whether the argument holds up. That check is called peer review. It does not make an article correct, but it does mean someone qualified looked at it first.

4
Judge the source
Four questions before you rely on anything
●
Finding a source on JSTOR is a good sign. It is not a verdict. Before you lean on it, ask it four questions.
1
Currency
When was it written?
2
Relevance
Does it answer your question, or only mention your topic?
3
Authority
Who wrote it, and where did it appear?
4
Purpose
Is it informing, arguing, or selling?

5
Write your opening and plan your plants
Project Feeder 3 · due in Week 5
●
Project Feeder 3 is your opening and your Foreshadowing Plan. Your opening is 150 to 300 words. It introduces the character and want from your Story Seed and leans toward your Dilemma without announcing it. Inside it you plant three details. Each one reads as ordinary on a first read and as a clue on a second.

A planted detail can be an object, a habit, a line of dialogue, or a setting detail. The detail you looked up can be one of them.

FOCUS QUESTION

What can your opening plant, in plain sight, that will read one way going in and another way looking back?

DIRECTIONS
  1. Write the opening of your story and plan the clues it plants. Reopen your Story Seed and Dilemma (shown above). The opening introduces the Seed’s character and want and points toward the Dilemma without announcing it.
  2. Write your opening paragraphs (150–300 words). Establish protagonist, setting, and mood with sense-reporting modifiers and controlled sentence rhythm. No verdict words.
  3. Plant three details inside the opening. Each reads as ordinary now and as a clue later. An object, a habit, a line of dialogue, or a setting detail all work.
  4. Below the opening, add your Foreshadowing Plan: a table with three rows and three columns: the detail I planted · what it quietly anticipates · where it pays off later in the story. Number the rows 1–3 to match the plants.
  5. Keep this work. Project Feeder 4 (Week 7) grows this opening into the full draft; the payoff column is the drafting map.
A MODEL PLAN, ON A DIFFERENT STORY
Detail planted: Mara checks the ferry lamp three times each night. What it anticipates: she checks what she cannot trust. Where it pays off: she checks the envelope seal three times before she decides.

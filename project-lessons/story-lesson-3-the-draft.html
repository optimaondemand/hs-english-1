This is the week you write the whole story, from your opening to the last line. Today you choose your ending, plan the middle so both costs show on the page, decide where irony goes, and pick the three techniques you will name in your craft reflection. Then you draft in Word.

1
Your three pieces and your ending
Choose it on purpose
●
You are not starting from scratch. You already have a Story Seed, a Dilemma, and an Opening with three plants. Today you add the middle: events that push your protagonist toward the choice, the choice itself, and an ending you picked on purpose.

Pick the ending your story can earn. Any of the three can be right. What is graded is that you chose it on purpose and built the story to deserve it.

Resolved
The knot is untied and the reader knows how it came out. Model: “The Gift of the Magi.”
A twist
The last turn changes what the reader thought they knew, and the groundwork was there. Model: “An Occurrence at Owl Creek Bridge.”
Deliberately ambiguous
The story stops on the question and trusts the reader. Model: “The Lady, or the Tiger?”
Why does this ending fit your story?
One or two sentences. Name what in your story makes it fair.

2
Build the middle
Pressure, then the choice
●
The middle has two jobs. First, pressure: events that push your protagonist toward the choice. Then the choice, with the cost of each option visible on the page. Your planted details go to work somewhere in here.
Pressure event 1: what happens that makes the choice closer?
Pressure event 2: what makes it harder?
Pressure event 3: what leaves no way out?
The choice scene: what does your protagonist choose, and what does it cost?
Show the cost in what happens and what they see, not in a sentence that explains it.
3
Dialogue that shows character
One revision pass
●
Revise your dialogue before you move on. Four moves turn lines that only trade information into lines that also show who is talking.
1
Their word
Swap the general word for the character's word.
2
Let them dodge
People rarely answer the question asked.
3
Cut the warm-up
Start where the meaning starts.
4
Action, not adverb
Give them something to do instead of “angrily.”
QUICK CHECK · NOT GRADED
Test every line: could anyone else in the story say this? Which line passes?
Write one line of dialogue only your protagonist could say
Put it in quotation marks, with a comma inside the closing mark if a tag follows.
4
Place your irony
One form, used for a reason
●
Irony does quiet work when it serves the story. Three forms: verbal irony, where words mean something other than what they say. Situational irony, where what happens is the opposite of what was expected. Dramatic irony, where the reader knows something a character does not.
"THE CASK OF AMONTILLADO" · A GREETING
"My dear Fortunato, you are luckily met. How remarkably well you are looking to-day!"
Edgar Allan Poe, 1846. Montresor greets the man he has vowed to ruin. Public domain.
QUICK CHECK · NOT GRADED
Why is Montresor's greeting ironic?

Which form will your story use? Choose the main one.

Where in your story does it appear, and what does it do for the reader?

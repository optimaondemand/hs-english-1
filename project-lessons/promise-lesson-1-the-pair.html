A pair at work
Gettysburg and The Scarlet Letter, as a model
●
Before you choose your own pair, watch one pair work. This model is on a different topic from the one you may choose. Copy the shape, not the ideas. First, the document: Lincoln's Gettysburg Address, one line.
GETTYSBURG ADDRESS · 1863
"dedicated to the proposition that all men are created equal"
Lincoln, speaking at a battlefield cemetery about the nation's founding promise
●
The story: The Scarlet Letter, in which a Puritan town in Boston judges Hester Prynne by its own moral law while it hides sins of its own. The model has a question, a thesis, and two paragraphs.
MODEL QUESTION
What does a community owe to the standard it uses to judge other people?
MODEL THESIS
Lincoln asks whether a nation can live up to its own founding promise, and Hawthorne shows a town enforcing a law on one woman while its leaders keep their own secrets, so together they argue that a standard means nothing unless the people who set it accept it too.
●
Now two model body paragraphs. One passes the cover test. One does not. The cover test: hide one of your two texts. If the paragraph still makes its point, it never used both.
PARAGRAPH A
Lincoln says the nation was dedicated to the idea that all people are created equal. He says the war tests whether such a nation can last. He honors the soldiers who died at Gettysburg and asks the living to finish their work. His speech is short, and he does not claim the nation has already succeeded.
PARAGRAPH B
Lincoln says a war is testing whether a nation built on equality can last, and Hawthorne's Boston runs a test of its own on Hester Prynne in the marketplace. In both texts the test is real. In both, the people applying it are also the people it should measure. Lincoln speaks as part of the nation under test, while Hawthorne's magistrates judge Hester and leave the man who shares her sin to keep his own secret. The difference tells you something: Lincoln puts himself inside the standard, and the town does not.
QUICK CHECK · NOT GRADED

What a researchable question looks like
Too broad, too small, just right
●
Your question decides the paper. If it is too wide, you can only summarize. If it is too small, one fact answers it. Run any question through these four rows (the same test you learned in Module 5).
Broadverdict
WAS THE AMERICAN REVOLUTION JUSTIFIED?
Libraries have been written on it. You could only summarize.
Smallverdict
WHAT YEAR WAS THE DECLARATION SIGNED?
One fact. This is a search, not research.
Answerableverdict
DID THE DECLARATION'S ARGUMENT ABOUT CONSENT SHAPE HOW THE CONSTITUTION HANDLED REPRESENTATION?
Narrow enough to research, open enough that informed people disagree.
Unsettledverdict
WAS JEFFERSON A GOOD PERSON?
No evidence could settle it. Rewrite it about actions or effects.

PURPOSE

The module project is a research-based argument and depends on the question chosen now: not so broad that it can only be summarized, not so narrow that it has one answer.

📚 PART 1 · WHAT A RESEARCHABLE QUESTION LOOKS LIKE

The Module 5 test applies to a bigger subject. Run any candidate question through these four rows:

The questionVerdict
Was the American Revolution justified?Too broad. Libraries have been written on it. You would only be able to summarize.
What year was the Declaration signed?Too small. One fact, no argument. This is a search, not research.
Did the Declaration's argument about consent influence how the Constitution handled representation?Answerable. Narrow enough to research, open enough that informed people disagree.
Was Jefferson a good person?Not researchable as written. No evidence could settle it. Rewrite it as a question about actions or effects.

Where good questions come from

Almost always from something that did not add up while reading. Two starting points from the grievances lesson: the document gives no reasons for the King's actions, and there is a gap between all men are created equal and who signed it. Either is the beginning of a real question.

🔗 CONNECTING TO YOUR PROJECT
Your final paper puts one of these documents in conversation with a work of literature you read this year — Electra, Sir Gawain, the Canterbury Tales, or a Semester 2 text. The literary text does not need to be named yet.

Find three candidate sources. They do not have to be the ones you end up using; this is a hunt, not a commitment. What matters is that each one is real, that you opened it yourself, and that you wrote the citation down at the moment you found it.

  1. Narrow a question. Turn something you wondered about into a question research could actually settle.
  2. Hunt three sources. One scholarly, one primary, one of your choosing — with citations written out.

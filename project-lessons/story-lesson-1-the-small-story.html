What a small story can hold
The six things your story must have
●
Your central question: what can a small story hold? In 1,000 to 1,500 words you put one person in front of a choice that costs something either way, plant details that pay off, let irony do quiet work, and end the story the way you mean to. Your story must have all six of these.
1
Length
1,000 to 1,500 words
2
A real dilemma
Cost on both sides
3
Foreshadowing
At least two planted details that pay off
4
Irony
At least one form: verbal, situational, or dramatic
5
A chosen ending
Resolved, twist, or deliberately open
6
Craft reflection
150 to 250 words, three techniques, then a Source Note

Also required: a real title, at least three Word Study terms used accurately in your craft reflection, a story that is entirely your own, and a proofread. You borrow techniques from the seven stories you read, never their plots or their words.

Plant your seed
Feeds Project Feeder 1 in Week 2
●
Project Feeder 1 is your Story Seed, about 150 to 250 words. A seed is the smallest thing that can grow into a story: a character, a want, and a price they never saw coming. Make the want concrete. Mr. White in "The Monkey's Paw" wanted two hundred pounds, not happiness.
A MODEL SEED, ON A DIFFERENT STORY
Mara works the night ferry and wants one thing: the seat in the harbor office that would let her stop working nights. A passenger leaves a sealed envelope and says to hand it to the harbor master only if she gets a fair price for it. She can sell it to a rival for a promotion, or deliver it and stay where she is. The cost she does not see: the rival is her brother.
Your protagonist: a name and one line about who they are
Example: Mara, a night-ferry deckhand.
What do they want badly?
One or two sentences. A thing you could point to, not a feeling.
The wish or choice that seems to grant it
A shortcut, a lie, a bargain, a new technology, or an enchanted object.
The cost they did not see coming
Two or three sentences. Why could they not see it?

Which kind of conflict is at the center? You studied four in Week 1.

One sentence: how might it end?
A guess is fine. You can change it.
FOCUS QUESTION

What does your character want badly enough to pay for, and what is the price they never saw coming?

DIRECTIONS
  1. Name your character and their want. One or two sentences on who the character is and what they want badly. Make the want concrete (Mr. White wanted two hundred pounds, not “happiness”).
  2. Give them the wish or choice that seems to grant it. It can be a shortcut, a lie, a bargain, a new technology, or an enchanted object. This is Jacobs’s move: the paw “twisted in my hand like a snake” at the very moment of wishing.
  3. Name the unintended cost. Two or three sentences on what getting the wish costs and why the character did not see it coming.
  4. Close with two labels: the story’s likely conflict type (from Week 1) and one sentence about how it might end.
Write your Story Seed here, about 150 to 250 words
Use the pieces you built above. Put the two labels at the end: your conflict type, then how it might end.
SUCCESS CRITERIA
Check each line before you download
A named character with one concrete want.
A wish or choice that grants the want, and an unintended cost that follows from it.
Conflict type labeled and one possible-ending sentence included.
About 150–250 words, proofread, named and saved correctly.

Completion grade: full credit for a complete, on-topic seed that meets the success criteria. Worth 15 points.

Project Feeder 2 is your Dilemma, about 250 to 300 words. In "The Lady, or the Tiger?" a princess must point her lover to one of two doors, and both doors cost her. Yours should too. Use at least one conditional sentence for each side: if she chooses A, then...; if she chooses B, then...
The choice, in one sentence
Make it two-sided: Option A or Option B. A colon can reveal it: She had two doors: ...
What Option A costs
One short paragraph.
What Option B costs
One short paragraph. It must hurt as much as the first.
Why is this choice hard for this character in particular?
Two or three sentences: name the two forces inside them that make both options live.

FOCUS QUESTION

What choice could your protagonist face that costs something real no matter which way they turn?

DIRECTIONS
  1. Reintroduce your protagonist from your Story Seed in 2–3 sentences: who they are and the one thing they want most.
  2. State the impossible choice in one sentence, and make it two-sided: Option A and Option B, like Stockton’s two doors. A colon reveal works well here (see the colons lesson).
  3. Name the cost of each option, one short paragraph per side. Use at least one conditional sentence per side (see the conditional sentences lesson): if she chooses A, then…; if she chooses B, then…
  4. Explain why the choice is hard for this particular character (2–3 sentences): name the two forces inside the protagonist that make both options live.
  5. Use at least three of this week’s Word Study terms accurately: ambiguity, semi-barbaric, impartial, fervent, deliberation.
Write your Dilemma here, about 250 to 300 words
Pull your pieces together in this order: protagonist, the two-sided choice, the cost of each option, why it is hard. Use at least three Word Study terms.
SUCCESS CRITERIA
Check each line before you download
A protagonist a reader can picture, and the one thing they want most.
One clear two-sided choice, with a real cost named for each side (conditional sentences used).
An explanation of why this character, specifically, finds both options live.
About 250–300 words, three Word Study terms used well, proofread, named and saved correctly.

Completion grade: full credit for a complete, on-topic response that meets the success criteria. Worth 15 points.

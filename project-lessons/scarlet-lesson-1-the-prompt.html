What the essay looks like
A literary analysis essay argues one idea about the novel and proves it with the novel's own words
●
Your essay has six parts. Every part has a job, and every part pushes the same single claim, called the thesis. The thesis is one sentence that answers your prompt and can be argued with.
THE ESSAY MUST HAVE
  • One thesis in the introduction.
  • At least three quotations from the novel, each introduced and cited by chapter, like this: (Hawthorne, ch. 6).
  • One researched source beyond the novel, used in your argument, cited in the text, and listed on a Works Cited page.
  • One counterclaim paragraph: the strongest view against yours, with a fair concession and a rebuttal.
  • MLA format: Times New Roman 12 point, double-spaced, your last name and page number in the header, a heading block, a title, and a Works Cited page.

Three prompts, one choice
You will write about one. Pick the one that pulls you.
●
Each prompt asks a question the novel does not answer for you. You will confirm your choice in Week 3, so choose the one you want to keep thinking about. You can still change your mind in The Evidence.
A. The letter
Hawthorne lets the meaning of the scarlet letter change. What changes it: the community or Hester?
B. The minister
Does Dimmesdale's public confession redeem him, or does it come too late?
C. The theme
What does a community owe the person it judges, and what does a person owe the truth? Argue what the novel answers.
Why this prompt?
One or two sentences. What about it made you want to argue it?

Where each prompt lives in the novel
Chapter numbers, so you know what to watch for as you read
●
You are reading Chapters I to VI this week, and the rest over the next three weeks. Keep your prompt in mind as you read. The moments you notice now will become your quotations in The Evidence.
WATCH FOR, BY PROMPT
  • A. The letter: the crowd at the scaffold (Chapters II and III), Hester's needlework and Pearl (Chapters V and VI), the town's changed view of her (Chapter XIII), and the forest (Chapters XVII to XIX).
  • B. The minister: his silence and his suffering (Chapters IX to XII), the forest meeting (Chapter XVII), and the scaffold and the Conclusion (Chapters XXIII and XXIV).
  • C. The theme: the hearing at the governor's hall (Chapters VII and VIII), Chillingworth's role (Chapters IX and X), and the whole arc of the town's judgment, start to finish.
One thing you have noticed already
Chapters I to III count if you have read them. One sentence about a person, a place, or a moment that seems to matter for your prompt.
6
A model paragraph, on a different claim
Read it once for what it says, once for how it is built
●
This model argues about Pearl, a character you will meet in Chapter VI, so it will not give you your own claim. Notice the four layers of the body paragraph below.
CHAPTER VI · PEARL
"The child could not be made amenable to rules. In giving her existence, a great law had been broken; and the result was a being whose elements were perhaps beautiful and brilliant, but all in disorder"
Hawthorne, The Scarlet Letter, ch. 6 (Project Gutenberg)
A MODEL BODY PARAGRAPH
Claim. Hawthorne shows that a town can punish a mother but cannot make her child obey its rules.
Lead-in. When the narrator describes Pearl's nature in Chapter VI, he admits that she does not fit the rules around her.
Quotation. He writes that "The child could not be made amenable to rules" (Hawthorne, ch. 6).
Explanation. The word "amenable" means willing to be guided. Hawthorne does not blame Pearl for refusing. He sets "beautiful and brilliant" beside "disorder," so the reader sees a child who is not bad but is simply beyond the town's reach.
QUICK CHECK · NOT GRADED
In the model, which sentence is the explanation?
7
A model counterclaim
Say what is true in the other view, then answer it
●
A counterclaim paragraph states the strongest view against your thesis. First it concedes what is true in that view. Then it rebuts it, using the novel.
CHAPTER VI · THE OTHER SIDE
"Pearl was a born outcast of the infantile world."
Hawthorne, The Scarlet Letter, ch. 6 (Project Gutenberg)
A MODEL COUNTERCLAIM (SAME CLAIM ABOUT PEARL)
The opposing view. A reader could say the town's rules do reach Pearl, because the other children shut her out.
Concession. That is true: the narrator says she "was a born outcast of the infantile world" (Hawthorne, ch. 6).
Rebuttal. But being shut out is not the same as being ruled. The children can keep her away, yet Chapter VI says nothing that shows she has taken their rules into herself.
What did the model concede, and what did it keep?
One or two sentences. This is the move you will make in Week 4.

📖 FOCUS
The same red A is a brand, a calling, a lie, and a legend. What changed: the letter or the readers?
The letter’s form is fixed; its meaning depends on who reads it and what they have seen. Hawthorne also organizes the novel around three scaffold scenes that show the same community and the same letter at three distances.

📚 PART 1 · THE LETTER’S CHANGING MEANINGS
💭 WARM-UP
Which moment from the novel are you most likely to write about?

The letter’s shape never changes: a red A in gold-thread. Its meaning is assigned by whoever reads it, and Hawthorne changes the reader more often than the letter. Five stages mark the change.

1
Chapter II: a public brand worn with defiance. Hawthorne describes “elaborate embroidery and fantastic flourishes of gold-thread” around the letter. The town assigns the meaning; Hester decorates it.
2
Chapter XIII: a calling. “The letter was the symbol of her calling.” Many people offer a new reading: “They said that it meant Able” The meaning shifts because of what Hester does.
3
Chapter XV: a denial. Pressed by Pearl, Hester answers “I wear it for the sake of its gold-thread.” Hawthorne notes that she had never before been false to the symbol.
4
Chapters XVIII–XIX: removed, then retaken. After she lifts it off, “The stigma gone, Hester heaved a long, deep sigh”. In Chapter XIX she “took up the scarlet letter, and fastened it again into her bosom”.
5
Chapter XXIV: a type of something to be revered. Over the years “the scarlet letter ceased to be a stigma”. The novel ends on the motto “ON A FIELD, SABLE, THE LETTER A, GULES.”
📚 PART 2 · THE THREE SCAFFOLD SCENES AS STRUCTURE

Hawthorne builds the novel on three scenes at the same platform. In the first, the community punishes Hester in daylight. In the second, Dimmesdale stands on the platform at night, where no one can judge him. In the third, he stands there in daylight before the same community.

THE THREE SCAFFOLD SCENES · SAME PLATFORM, THREE DISTANCES
FIRST SCAFFOLD · CHAPTERS II–III
ON THE PLATFORM
Hester, holding Pearl.
WATCHING
The whole town. Dimmesdale looks on from the balcony and joins the call for her to name the father.
THE LETTER IS
A public punishment, embroidered by Hester in gold-thread.
DIMMESDALE
Silent about his own guilt.
SECOND SCAFFOLD · CHAPTER XII
ON THE PLATFORM
Dimmesdale, alone in the dark; then Hester and Pearl join him.
WATCHING
Almost no one. Father Wilson passes with a lantern; Chillingworth appears at the foot of the platform.
THE LETTER IS
A secret held by three people, with no public reading.
DIMMESDALE
Pearl says he would not promise to stand with them at noontide.
THIRD SCAFFOLD · CHAPTER XXIII
ON THE PLATFORM
Dimmesdale, Hester, and Pearl; Chillingworth kneels beside Dimmesdale at the end.
WATCHING
The whole town.
THE LETTER IS
Reread by Dimmesdale as “but the shadow of what he bears on his own breast”.
DIMMESDALE
Calls Hester to his side and bares his breast in public.
The first and third scenes are public and frame the private second scene. Dimmesdale moves from the balcony, to the platform at night, to the platform in daylight.

The second scene sets up the third. In Chapter XII Pearl says to Dimmesdale “Thou wouldst not promise to take my hand, and mother’s hand, to-morrow noontide!” In Chapter XXIII he takes their hands before the town. An essay on the whole novel can use these scenes as evidence of a pattern: private guilt moves, scene by scene, toward public truth.

📚 WORD STUDY
stigma: a mark of disgrace.  ·  penitence: sorrow for wrongdoing that leads to action.  ·  ambiguous: open to more than one reading.
Word origins: stigma comes from Greek stigma, a mark pricked into the skin; penitence comes from Latin paenitere, “to repent”; ambiguous comes from Latin ambigere, “to wander about, be uncertain.”

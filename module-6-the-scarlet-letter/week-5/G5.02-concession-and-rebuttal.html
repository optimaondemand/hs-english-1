📖 FOCUS
An essay is stronger when it states the best opposing reading and then answers it.
Concession and rebuttal sentences grant a point and then answer it. They rely on subordinating conjunctions, semicolons, and the transition however.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can write a concession and rebuttal sentence with correct punctuation.
2
I can avoid double conjunctions and comma splices in counterclaim sentences.
3
I can answer an opposing reading with evidence from the novel.
📋 MATERIALS
▶
The novel: Chapter XXIII and Chapter XXIV supply the examples (public domain, free to read online).

📚 PART 1 · WARM-UP
💭 READ BOTH ALOUD

Which writer sounds more persuasive?
📚 PART 2 · THE LESSON

A counterclaim is the strongest reading that opposes the writer’s thesis. Answering it makes the essay more convincing. The sentence tools are two: a concession that grants what is true in the opposing reading, and a rebuttal that answers it with evidence.

FOUR FRAMES FOR CONCESSION AND REBUTTAL
Although [concession], [rebuttal].  ·  While [concession], [rebuttal].  ·  It is true that [concession]; however, [rebuttal].  ·  Some readers [concession]; yet [rebuttal].
The concession is a fair statement of the opposing reading. The rebuttal is the writer’s answer, and it carries the weight.
ON THE BOARD · CONCESSION THEN REBUTTAL
WRONGAlthough Dimmesdale confesses in Chapter XXIII, but some witnesses deny seeing any mark on his breast.
RIGHTAlthough Dimmesdale confesses in Chapter XXIII, some witnesses deny seeing any mark on his breast.
Although already marks the contrast. Adding but builds a second conjunction and breaks the sentence.
WRONGIt is true that the town punishes Hester, however she changes the letter’s meaning.
RIGHTIt is true that the town punishes Hester; however, she changes the letter’s meaning through her work for others.
However is not a conjunction. A semicolon before it and a comma after it join the two clauses.
WRONGSome readers see Hester’s return as defeat. Those readers are mistaken.
RIGHTSome readers see Hester’s return as defeat; yet she resumes the letter “of her own free will” (Hawthorne, ch. 24).
A rebuttal answers with evidence, not with a verdict on the other readers.
The first example concerns Chapter XXIII and the conclusion in Chapter XXIV. The quotation is copied from the chapter text.

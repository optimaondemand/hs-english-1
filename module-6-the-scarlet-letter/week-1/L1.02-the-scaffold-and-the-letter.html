LESSON
The Scaffold and the Letter (Ch. I–III)
Reading lesson · the prison-door, the market-place, and the refusal
📖 FOCUS
Hester Prynne stands alone on a platform while a crowd, a governor, ministers, and a stranger look on. Who has power over her, and who sees her clearly?
Chapters I to III build one scene: the prison-door, the walk to the market-place, and the scaffold. This lesson maps who stands where and traces how one object, the letter A, is read by different people.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can map who holds authority over Hester in the scaffold scene.
2
I can compare how different characters and the narrator read the scarlet letter.
3
I can explain what Hester’s refusal to name the father shows about her and about the officials.

The crowd
The hard-featured women who want a harsher sentence · the young wife who pities Hester · schoolboys with a half-holiday · the town-beadle clearing the way.
At the edge of the crowd: a small, scholarly stranger who notices Hester’s recognition and lays a finger on his lips. Chapter IV names him Roger Chillingworth.
Height tracks authority: the officials stand highest, Hester stands exposed in the middle, and the crowd and the stranger stay below.

The prison-door and the rose-bush (Chapter I)

Hawthorne begins before Hester appears. Any new colony, he says, needs a burial-ground and a prison, and the Boston prison has already stained and rusted. Its yard bears “the black flower of civilized society, a prison”. Beside the door a wild rose-bush blooms, and the narrator offers a rose to the reader as something that “may serve, let us hope, to symbolize some sweet moral blossom”. He does not settle where the rose-bush came from: “we shall not take upon us to determine”. The chapter sets a prison against a flower and leaves the contrast open.

One letter, four readings (Chapter II)

On Hester’s breast the letter A appears “in fine red cloth, surrounded with an elaborate embroidery and fantastic flourishes of gold-thread”. The embroidery is her own work, and it is lavish. Each observer reads it differently.

Who reads the letterWhat it means to themIn Chapter II
The hard-featured womenA brazen display of pride that mocks the magistrates.“make a pride out of what they, worthy gentlemen, meant for a punishment”
The youngest woman in the crowdA private wound, felt stitch by stitch.“Not a stitch in that embroidered letter but she has felt it in her heart.”
The town-beadleThe law made visible.“where iniquity is dragged out into the sunshine”
The narratorA spell that isolates its wearer.“enclosing her in a sphere by herself”

The narrator’s reading goes furthest: the letter “had the effect of a spell, taking her out of the ordinary relations with humanity”. A punishment meant to bind Hester to the community separates her from it.

The refusal (Chapter III)

Governor Bellingham tells Dimmesdale that “the responsibility of this woman’s soul lies greatly with you”, and Wilson tells him to “Exhort her to confess the truth!” Dimmesdale appeals to Hester from the balcony, and Wilson repeats the demand. Hester answers “Never!”, looking “not at Mr. Wilson, but into the deep and troubled eyes of the younger clergyman,” and adds, “would that I might endure his agony, as well as mine!” When a voice from the crowd orders her to give her child a father, she answers, “I will not speak!” Chapter III never states the father’s name; the details of who is addressed and who watches carry the meaning.

The stranger voices a complaint of his own: “the partner of her iniquity should not, at least, stand on the scaffold by her side”. The scene asks who is on the scaffold and who is not.

📚 WORD STUDY
pillory: a wooden platform and frame on which an offender was displayed to the public.  ·  sumptuary: regulating personal spending, as laws that limited luxury in dress.  ·  malefactress: a woman who commits a crime or wrong.  ·  token: a sign or symbol of something.
Word origins: Sumptuary comes from Latin sumptus, “expense.” Malefactress joins Latin male, “badly,” and facere, “to do.” Token comes from Old English tæcen, “sign,” the root of teach.
💪 THE ONE THING TO CARRY FORWARD

The scaffold scene is organized by height and gaze. One letter carries as many meanings as there are observers, and the narrator’s own reading is the one that questions the whole arrangement.

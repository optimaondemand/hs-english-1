READING LESSON
The Leech and His Patient (Ch. IX–XII)
Character change, hypocrisy, and the second scaffold
📖 FOCUS
Chillingworth begins as a man seeking the truth. At what point does the search become revenge, and what does the minister's silence cost him?
Chapters IX to XII follow a physician who becomes a tormentor and a minister who is honored for sins he hides. The craft focus is how Hawthorne changes a character through shifting images, and how the night scene on the scaffold repeats and reverses the first.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can trace the images that show Chillingworth's change from judge to avenger.
2
I can explain the gap between Dimmesdale's private guilt and his public reputation.
3
I can compare the night scene on the scaffold with Hester's public punishment.

📚 PART 2 · FROM JUDGE TO AVENGER

Chillingworth begins his investigation, in the narrator's words, “with the severe and equal integrity of a judge, desirous only of truth”, even though the question involves “wrongs inflicted on himself”. He has been wronged, and the novel never denies it. The question is what he does with the wrong. Hawthorne answers through images, and the chart shows the five that carry the change.

HOW THE NARRATOR PICTURES CHILLINGWORTH · FIVE IMAGES, IN ORDER (CH. X–XI)
1
THE JUDGE
“with the severe and equal integrity of a judge, desirous only of truth”
A lawful, fair inquiry. This is how Chillingworth sees his own purpose.
2
THE MINER
“like a miner searching for gold”
The inquiry becomes digging. The heart is a mine, and the patient is the ground.
3
THE SEXTON
“like a sexton delving into a grave”
Digging among the dead. The narrator adds that he is likely to find only corruption.
4
THE THIEF
“as a thief entering a chamber where a man lies only half asleep”
Stealth and a victim. The inquiry is now a theft.
5
THE ACTOR
“not a spectator only, but a chief actor, in the poor minister’s interior world”
He no longer watches. He causes: the narrator says “He could play upon him as he chose”.
The images move from an office (judge) to digging (miner, sexton) to crime (thief) to control (actor). Hawthorne shows the change through the pictures he chooses.

By Chapter XI the narrator names what has happened: “a quiet depth of malice, hitherto latent, but active now”. A later passage calls Chillingworth “more wretched than his victim”. The narrator condemns the method and still pities the man.

📚 PART 3 · THE TWO DIMMESDALES

The town sees a saint. In private, Dimmesdale keeps a scourge, fasts until his knees tremble, and holds vigils before a mirror. The gap between these two men is the engine of Chapters X and XI.

The argument about confession

In Chapter X, Chillingworth presses the point through a plant growing on a nameless grave. Of men who hide guilt, he says “They fear to take up the shame that rightfully belongs to them.” Dimmesdale defends the silent, then watches Hester and Pearl pass and concedes “it must needs be better for the sufferer to be free to show his pain, as this poor woman Hester is, than to cover it all up in his heart.” He states the principle that condemns him.

Confession that fails

Dimmesdale imagines the whole truth, spoken from the pulpit: “I, your pastor, whom you so reverence and trust, am utterly a pollution and a lie!” When he does speak, he tells his hearers he is altogether vile, with no particulars. The congregation draws the opposite conclusion: “The godly youth!” “The saint on earth!” The narrator's verdict is exact: “He had spoken the very truth, and transformed it into the veriest falsehood.” General confession of unworthiness is a recognized pose of humility, and it earns praise. Hawthorne calls Dimmesdale a “subtle, but remorseful hypocrite”.

📚 PART 4 · THE SECOND SCAFFOLD

Chapter XII returns to the platform where Hester stood in Chapter II. It is the second of three scaffold scenes, and each reverses the one before. Hester stood at noon before the whole town, by sentence of the court. Dimmesdale climbs at midnight, alone, by choice: “There was no peril of discovery.” The narrator calls the act a trial of conscience with no witness: “Was it but the mockery of penitence?”

Hester and Pearl pass by, and he calls them up. Pearl asks the question the town cannot ask: “Wilt thou stand here with mother and me, to-morrow noontide?” He answers “At the great judgment day” and adds “But the daylight of this world shall not see our meeting!” He will stand with them in the dark and never in the light. When he refuses, the verdict of the child is blunt:“Thou wast not bold!”

A meteor then shapes a red letter A in the sky, and the narrator refuses to confirm it: “We impute it, therefore, solely to the disease in his own eye and heart”. The letter is a symbol again, this time projected by a guilty mind, so that “another’s guilt might have seen another symbol in it”. The next morning the sexton returns Dimmesdale's glove, found on the scaffold, with a remark: “A pure hand needs no glove to cover it!” The sexton intends a compliment, and the reader hears the opposite. The town's trust is the thing that keeps the secret safe.

📚 WORD STUDY
hypocrite: one who performs a virtue he does not have  ·  remorse: deep regret for a wrong done  ·  vigil: a period of watching or staying awake  ·  penance: an act of self-punishment to show repentance  ·  malice: a wish to do harm
Word origins: Hypocrite comes from Greek hypokrites, “actor, stage player”: a hypocrite plays a part. Remorse comes from Latin remordere, “to bite again”: remorse is a second bite. Vigil comes from Latin vigilia, “wakefulness.” Penance comes from Latin paenitentia, “repentance,” the root of penitent. Malice comes from Latin malus, “bad.” Copia: Rewrite the sentence The doctor watched the minister twice: once so the doctor seems kind, once so he seems predatory. Change no facts.

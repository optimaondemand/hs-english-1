READING LESSON
The Governor's Hall and the Elf-Child (Ch. VII–VIII)
Symbol, motif, and irony
📖 FOCUS
A governor, two ministers, and a mother argue over one child. What does the argument show about how the town sees the scarlet letter?
Hester goes to Governor Bellingham's mansion to keep Pearl. The scene rests on a symbol (a concrete thing that stands for an idea) and on three competing readings of what the letter means.

3

📚 PART 2 · THE LETTER AS A SYMBOL

A symbol is a concrete thing that stands for an idea. A motif is a symbol or image that returns. The scarlet letter is the novel's central symbol, and Chapter VII multiplies it. Hester dresses Pearl in crimson velvet and gold thread, and the narrator says plainly what the dress is:

It was the scarlet letter in another form; the scarlet letter endowed with life!

Pearl is the letter made alive. The children of the town read her that way at once, and they act on it: “let us fling mud at them!” They see the badge, then the woman, then the child.

Inside the hall, Pearl looks into the Governor's polished armor. Hester looks too, and finds the letter distorted:

the scarlet letter was represented in exaggerated and gigantic proportions, so as to be greatly the most prominent feature of her appearance.

Hawthorne adds one more clause: “she seemed absolutely hidden behind it.” The curved steel makes the town's way of seeing into an image. The letter grows until it hides the person wearing it. This is the question the hearing will argue.

📚 PART 3 · THE HEARING, ARGUMENT BY ARGUMENT

Chapter VIII stages an argument with a verdict. Each speaker offers a different answer to what the letter means, and the chart shows where each one stands.

THE HEARING IN THE HALL · FOUR VOICES, ONE QUESTION: WHO SHOULD RAISE PEARL?
GOVERNOR BELLINGHAM
the magistrate
Asks whether a woman who has fallen can be trusted with an immortal soul. Rests on the letter as proof of unfitness.
“Woman, it is thy badge of shame!”
MR. WILSON
the senior minister
Tests Pearl on the catechism. Rests on her answer as proof of neglect.
“Canst thou tell me, my child, who made thee?”
HESTER
the mother
Argues from experience: the letter has taught her something Pearl can use.
“I can teach my little Pearl what I have learned from this!”
MR. DIMMESDALE
the young minister
Reframes the question: Pearl is a gift given to keep her mother's soul alive.
“It was meant for a blessing; for the one blessing of her life!”
Outcome: the Governor leaves the matter as it stands, “so long, at least, as there shall be no further scandal in the woman.” The decision follows Dimmesdale's argument, not Hester's.

Pearl's answer

Wilson's question gets a startling reply. Pearl, the narrator reports, “had not been made at all, but had been plucked by her mother off the bush of wild roses that grew by the prison-door”. The Governor takes this as proof of ignorance: “Methinks, gentlemen, we need inquire no further.” The narrator has already said Pearl knows her catechism and is being perverse. The test measures a child's mood, and the Governor treats it as a measure of her soul.

Dimmesdale and irony

Hester turns to the young minister: “Speak thou for me!” He answers with the strongest argument in the scene, ending with a line that is easy to pass over: “Herein is the sinful mother happier than the sinful father.” Roger Chillingworth, standing nearby, remarks with a smile that Dimmesdale speaks with “a strange earnestness”.

Irony is a gap between what is said and what is meant, or between what a character knows and what the reader suspects. Dimmesdale pleads for a woman whose sin the reader suspects he shares. The first reading finds a kind defender; the second hears a man arguing for himself. Chapters X to XII settle what Chapter VIII only suggests.

The invitation

As Hester leaves, Mistress Hibbins, the Governor's sister, invites her to a meeting in the forest. Hester refuses, and adds: “Had they taken her from me, I would willingly have gone with thee into the forest, and signed my name in the Black Man’s book too, and that with mine own blood!” The narrator draws the conclusion: “Even thus early had the child saved her from Satan’s snare.” The hearing was never only about custody. Taking Pearl would have taken the one tie that held Hester inside the community.

📚 WORD STUDY
symbol: a concrete thing that stands for an idea  ·  motif: a symbol or image that returns  ·  irony: a gap between what is said and what is meant  ·  retribution: punishment given in return for a wrong  ·  ignominy: public disgrace
Word origins: symbol comes from Greek symbolon, a token broken in two so that the halves could later be matched; a symbol is half of a pair, and the reader supplies the other. Irony comes from Greek eironeia, pretended ignorance. Retribution comes from Latin retribuere, “to pay back.” Ignominy comes from Latin in-, “not,” and nomen, “name”: a loss of one's good name. Copia: Rewrite the sentence The woman wore a badge twice: once so the badge is a punishment, once so it is a symbol of something larger.

RESEARCH LESSON
Sorting Claims About the Puritans
Source types, the four-question check, bias by omission, and MLA
📖 FOCUS
Two sources describe the same people and disagree. How does a reader decide which claims to carry into an essay?
Claims about the Puritans have been argued over for centuries. This lesson sorts three kinds of source (scholarly article, encyclopedia entry, popular web page), applies the four-question check and the test for bias by omission, and sets the habit of recording an MLA entry at the moment a source is found.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can sort a source as scholarly, reference, or popular and say what each is best used for.
2
I can apply the four-question check and name one omission in a source.
3
I can record an MLA entry at the moment I find a source.

📚 PART 1 · WHY CLAIMS ABOUT THE PURITANS DIFFER
💭 WARM-UP
A search for the Puritans returns three results: the abstract of a scholarly article on town government in early Massachusetts, an encyclopedia entry, and a page that lists strange Puritan rules. Which opens first?

Claims about the Puritans disagree because sources differ in four ways: when they were written, for whom, from what evidence, and about which Puritans. Massachusetts Bay was settled by English Protestants in the 1630s, and the people, places, and decades involved are many. A claim about ministers in Boston does not automatically hold for farmers in a small town.

The Scarlet Letter is itself a source of this kind. Hawthorne published it in 1850, about two centuries after the events it imagines, and he writes with a view of his own. In Chapter VIII the narrator pushes back on a stereotype, calling it an error to suppose that the “grave forefathers” “made it a matter of conscience to reject such means of comfort, or even luxury, as lay fairly within their grasp.” The novel shows how the Puritans were imagined and argued over in 1850, and it is not a record of what happened in the 1640s.

📚 PART 2 · THREE KINDS OF SOURCE

Finding a source is a good start and not a verdict. Before relying on one, ask it the four questions from Module 5: currency, relevance, authority, purpose. The chart shows what scholarly articles, encyclopedia entries, and popular web pages tend to give for each.

THREE KINDS OF SOURCE · THE SAME FOUR QUESTIONS · WHAT EACH TENDS TO GIVE
QuestionScholarly articleEncyclopedia entryPopular web page
CurrencyThe date is printed. Newer work answers older work, though a classic study can stay sound.Editors update entries. Check the revision or copyright date.Often undated or recycled. A fresh date may mean a page was refreshed, not re-researched.
RelevanceNarrow: one argument about one question. The abstract settles relevance in a minute.Broad: the outline of a topic, rarely the scene or question needed.Varies. Built around a catchy topic, not around a research question.
AuthorityA named scholar, peer review, and notes that point to evidence.Credited editors and contributors; sources are often listed at the end.The author may be unnamed. Look up the site and author somewhere else.
PurposeArgues a claim to other scholars.Informs a general reader.Attracts clicks or sells. May flatten or exaggerate to do it.
Best useEvidence for an argument.Orientation, plus names and terms to search next.A claim to check, never the evidence itself.
These are tendencies. A given source can break its type, so the four questions are asked of each source, not of the type.

The Authority question is answered away from the source. A site's own About page was written by the site. Open a new tab, search for the author or organization, and see who funds them, what else they publish, and who cites them.

📚 PART 3 · BIAS BY OMISSION

Most bias is not a false statement. It is something true that was left out. A source can be accurate in every sentence and still mislead by never mentioning the evidence that cuts the other way. Three sample passages follow, each describing the same people.

SAMPLE PASSAGES, WRITTEN FOR THIS LESSON · NOT TAKEN FROM REAL PAGES
SAMPLE A · SCHOLARLY STYLE
Court records from early Massachusetts show many cases of debt, property, and quarrels between neighbors. They also record harsh punishments for moral offenses, which were public and meant to be seen.
SAMPLE B · REFERENCE STYLE
The Puritans were English Protestants who settled in Massachusetts in the 1630s. They sought to reform the Church of England, and in Massachusetts church membership shaped civic life.
SAMPLE C · POPULAR STYLE
The Puritans banned fun, dressed only in black, and punished anyone who stepped out of line. Their strict laws ruled every minute of daily life.

Sample C overclaims with absolute words (banned, only, every) and leaves out learning, local government, and the church's role in civic life. Harvard College was founded in 1636, and a Massachusetts law of 1647 required towns to provide schools. These facts do not make the Puritans gentle. They show that the picture is larger than the passage claims. Sample B is accurate and thin, and Sample A is narrow and careful. A second source on anything that matters shows the hole in the first.

📚 PART 4 · RECORD THE ENTRY WHEN YOU FIND IT

MLA lists a source by its core elements, in a fixed order. Record them while the page is open. Retracing a source later wastes time and invites errors.

Source typeMLA pattern
Scholarly articleLastname, Firstname. “Title of Article.” Title of Publication, vol. #, no. #, year, pp. #–#. JSTOR, stable URL.
Reference entry or web pageLastname, Firstname (if given). “Title of Entry.” Title of Website or Reference Work, Publisher, day month year, URL.

Capture at the moment of finding: author, title, publication or site, date, and location (the stable URL or DOI, copied without http:// or https://). The date a page was opened is optional in MLA and useful for pages that change. Titles of articles and entries go in quotation marks; the larger container is italic.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI AND SOURCES
An AI tool can suggest search terms for a question about Puritan New England. It can also produce citations with real-sounding authors and titles that do not exist. A source belongs in the project only if the student opened it and can produce it on request.
📚 WORD STUDY
credible: worth believing, on evidence  ·  omission: something true that was left out  ·  citation: a record that names a source and tells where to find it  ·  container: the larger work that holds an article or entry
Word origins: Credible comes from Latin credere, “to believe,” which also gives credit and creed. Omission comes from Latin omittere, “to let go”: the fact was let go. Citation comes from Latin citare, “to summon”: to cite a source is to call it forward as a witness. Copia: Rewrite the sentence The Puritans built a school twice: once so it sounds like praise, once so it sounds like an admission. Change no facts.

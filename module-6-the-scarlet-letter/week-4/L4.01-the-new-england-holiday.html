Lesson · Chapters XX, XXI, and XXII
🎯 FOCUS
Hester and Dimmesdale have agreed to leave together. Why does the town look strange to him, and why does Election Day make their situation worse?
Chapter XX follows Dimmesdale from the forest to his study. Chapters XXI and XXII place Hester, Pearl, and Chillingworth in the Election Day crowd while Dimmesdale preaches. Hawthorne puts one scarlet letter on view in the market-place and sets another, hidden, in the pulpit.

📚 PART 1 · THE MINISTER IN A MAZE (CHAPTER XX)
💭 WARM-UP
After a big decision that no one else knows about, familiar places usually feel…

Dimmesdale leaves the forest with a plan. A ship bound for Bristol sails on the fourth day, and Hester has arranged passage for two adults and a child. He is glad of the timing for a second reason, which Hawthorne exposes: on the third day he is to preach the Election Sermon, and he thinks of leaving with his public record clean. His thought is “that I leave no public duty unperformed, nor ill performed!” The narrator calls this a self-deception and states the principle behind it: “No man, for any considerable period, can wear one face to himself, and another to the multitude, without finally getting bewildered as to which may be the true.”

On the walk home, his old moral code gives way to impulses. He struggles not to say blasphemous things to a deacon. The only thing he can think to tell a devout widow is an argument against the immortality of the soul. He hides his face in his cloak to avoid the youngest church member. He wants to teach wicked words to small children and to trade oaths with a drunken sailor. Hawthorne’s explanation is that the sin “had stupefied all blessed impulses, and awakened into vivid life the whole brotherhood of bad ones.”

Mistress Hibbins, the reputed witch, greets him as a fellow traveler to the forest. In his study Chillingworth offers medicine and Dimmesdale refuses it. Dimmesdale then burns the pages of the sermon he had begun and writes a new one through the night.

📚 PART 2 · ELECTION DAY (CHAPTERS XXI–XXII)

Chapter XXI sets the holiday. The Puritans allow themselves one day of public mirth, and Hawthorne contrasts it with the festivals their English fathers remembered. Hester wears her usual gray. Hawthorne suggests that on this last day she faces the crowd freely, and that she might say, “Look your last on the scarlet letter and its wearer!”

The turn comes in the market-place. The shipmaster tells Hester that Chillingworth has arranged to sail on the same ship. Across the square Chillingworth smiles at her. The plan that offered escape now carries her tormentor with it.

ELECTION DAY · TWO PLACES, ONE MARK
THE MARKET-PLACE
Hester
Outside the church, at the foot of the scaffold (Ch. XXII)
Plain gray clothing, scarlet letter in full view
Space around her: “a sort of magic circle”
Stared at by townspeople, country visitors, and sailors
Hears the sermon only as a murmur through the walls
THE PULPIT
Dimmesdale
Inside the church, at the height of his reputation
Strong step; “nor did his hand rest ominously upon his heart”
Met by “almost worshipping respect”
Mind elsewhere: “But where was his mind?”
The mark on his breast is hidden
THE NARRATOR PUTS THEM SIDE BY SIDE (CH. XXII)
“The sainted minister in the church!” “The woman of the scarlet letter in the market-place!” “What imagination would have been irreverent enough to surmise that the same scorching stigma was on them both!”

In Chapter XXII Dimmesdale walks in the procession with new energy, and Hester sees how far he has withdrawn from her: “She hardly knew him now!” Pearl asks the question Hester cannot answer in public: “was that the same minister that kissed me by the brook?” Mistress Hibbins speaks the suspicion aloud: “What is it that the minister seeks to hide, with his hand always over his heart?”

Hester stands at the foot of the scaffold while the sermon goes on. Under its eloquence the narrator hears one cry of pain, a “complaint of a human heart, sorrow-laden, perchance guilty, telling its secret”.

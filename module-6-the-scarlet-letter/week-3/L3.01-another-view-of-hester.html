📚 PART 1 · THE TOWN’S VIEW
💭 WARM-UP
A person wears a mark of shame for seven years, never complains, and helps in every sickroom in town. What should a community do?

The town’s view

By Chapter XIII Pearl is seven. Hawthorne reports that “a species of general regard had ultimately grown up in reference to Hester Prynne”. Hester neither fights the public nor asks it for anything, and her work among the sick is steady. The narrator’s explanation is a judgment on communities: “The public is despotic in its temper”. It withholds justice when justice is demanded as a right, and often grants more than justice when the appeal is made to its generosity.

That distinction matters for the module’s question. The town’s mercy arrives as a favor it chooses to give, not as something it owes.

ONE LETTER, THREE READINGS · CHAPTER XIII
THE PEOPLE
A for Able
“They said that it meant Able”. Private citizens read the letter as the token of “her many good deeds since”.
THE RULERS
A sign to be read slowly
The magistrates “were longer in acknowledging the influence of Hester’s good qualities than the people”. In Chapter XIV a council debate over removing the letter is reported, and left undecided.
HESTER
Unfinished
“The scarlet letter had not done its office.” She asks whether she failed in “truth, courage, and loyalty” toward Dimmesdale.
The letter does not change across seven years. The readings of it do.
📚 PART 2 · HESTER AND CHILLINGWORTH

The meeting with Chillingworth (Chapter XIV)

Hester stops the physician while Pearl plays at the water’s edge. She tells him she means to end the secrecy: “I must reveal the secret”. Her reason is a debt: “this long debt of confidence, due from me to him”. She also names her own fault. Asked who made Chillingworth what he is, she answers “It was myself!”

Chillingworth admits what revenge has made of him, and Hester asks him to forgive. He declines, and moves the weight onto fate: “It is not granted me to pardon.” “It is our fate.” Hester takes the responsibility upon herself; Chillingworth hands it to necessity.

📚 PART 3 · HESTER AND PEARL

Pearl’s questions (Chapter XV)

After he leaves, Hester admits she hates him: “I hate the man!” Pearl, meanwhile, has made a green letter A of eel-grass for her own bosom. She asks what the scarlet letter means, and why “does the minister keep his hand over his heart”. Hester answers: “I wear it for the sake of its gold-thread.”

The narrator marks the moment: “In all the seven bygone years, Hester Prynne had never before been false to the symbol on her bosom.” The letter had been the one thing she refused to lie about. Her lie protects Dimmesdale, and it costs her the one standard she had kept.

📚 WORD STUDY
ignominy: public shame or disgrace  ·  benign: kindly; gentle  ·  penance: an act of suffering or repair for a wrong  ·  retribution: punishment given in return for a wrong
Word origins: Ignominy comes from Latin ignominia, built on nomen, “name”: the loss of one’s good name. Benign comes from Latin benignus, “kindly.” Penance comes from Latin paenitentia, “repentance,” which also gives penitence. Retribution comes from Latin retribuere, “to pay back.”
💪 KEY POINT

The town now reads the letter as Able; Hester reads it as unfinished business. Chapter XV shows what the unfinished business is: the letter has been her one standard of truth, and the secret she keeps for Dimmesdale has just broken it.

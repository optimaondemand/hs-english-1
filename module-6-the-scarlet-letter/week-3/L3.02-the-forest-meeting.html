READING LESSON
The Forest Meeting (Ch. XVI–XIX)
The secret told, the plan, and the letter in the leaves
📖 FOCUS
The forest stands outside the town’s law. Does leaving the law also free Hester and Dimmesdale from what they owe?
Chapters XVI to XIX move the novel out of the settlement. Hester tells Dimmesdale the truth about Chillingworth, proposes flight, and sets the letter aside; Pearl refuses the change. The narrator comments on every step.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can place the six turns of the forest scene in order.
2
I can explain how the narrator describes Hester’s and Dimmesdale’s different roads to the same decision.
3
I can use the brook, the sunshine, and the letter as evidence for what the scene says about freedom and truth.
📋 MATERIALS
▶
Chapter XVI, XVII, XVIII, and XIX of The Scarlet Letter (public domain; about 40 minutes)
📚 PART 1 · THE SCENE IN ORDER
💭 WARM-UP
In the forest there is no magistrate, no pulpit, and no crowd. What does a person say first?

Before the reading: what the forest stands for

In the settlement, the scaffold, the pulpit, and the governor’s hall enforce the law. The forest is the place beyond them. Hester says of the path that it leads backward to the settlement, but also onward into the wilderness, and Hawthorne treats the forest as a place where the usual authorities do not reach.

THE FOREST MEETING · SIX TURNS, IN ORDER
XVI
Pearl’s story of the Black Man
Pearl repeats a tale that the scarlet letter is the Black Man’s mark. Hester answers, “Once in my life I met the Black Man!” Dimmesdale appears on the path, hand over his heart.
XVII
The secret told
Dimmesdale names his despair, and wishes for one friend who knows him as he is: “Even thus much of truth would save me!” Hester tells him: “he was my husband!” His first answer is “I cannot forgive thee!” Then: “I do forgive you, Hester”.
XVII
The plan
Hester proposes flight by forest-track or sea. Dimmesdale says he is powerless to go. She answers, “Thou shalt not go alone!”
XVIII
The letter thrown aside
Dimmesdale resolves to flee. Hester says “Let us not look back”, unclasps the letter, and throws it among the leaves. The clouds break: “forth burst the sunshine”.
XIX
The child at the brook
Pearl will not cross. She points at her mother’s bosom and says “Come thou and take it up!” Hester fastens the letter on again.
XIX
The kiss
Pearl crosses: “Now thou art my mother indeed!” She kisses the letter too, then washes Dimmesdale’s kiss from her forehead in the brook.
📚 PART 2 · THE NARRATOR’S JUDGMENT

The narrator’s two roads (Chapter XVIII)

Chapter XVIII pauses to explain how each of them arrives at the same decision. Hester has been outside the community for seven years, and its shame, despair, and solitude were her teachers: “they had made her strong, but taught her much amiss”. Dimmesdale never left the law. His fall was “a sin of passion, not of principle, nor even purpose”, and he has watched his own conscience ever since.

The narrator does not praise the plan and does not condemn it. He weighs it. Dimmesdale is choosing “between fleeing as an avowed criminal, and remaining as a hypocrite”, and conscience “might find it hard to strike the balance”. He also adds a warning about guilt in general: “the breach which guilt has once made into the human soul is never, in this mortal state, repaired”.

Nature responds, with a qualification

When the letter is thrown aside the clouds break. The narrator calls this “the sympathy of Nature”, but qualifies it: Nature here is “wild, heathen Nature of the forest, never subjugated by human law, nor illumined by higher truth”. The sunshine shares their joy. It does not approve their choice.

Pearl at the brook (Chapter XIX)

Pearl stands across the brook, with a reflection of herself in the pool that points as she does. Hester explains the refusal: “Pearl misses something which she has always seen me wear!” The letter is picked up and refastened, and the narrator draws the lesson: “an evil deed invests itself with the character of doom”. Hester threw the letter away for an hour. The forest could not hold it there.

✎ CHECK YOURSELF · PICK THE BEST ANSWER
1. Why does Pearl refuse to cross the brook?
2. When Hester throws the letter aside, the narrator has the forest respond with…
3. What does the narrator say about the breach that guilt makes in the soul?
📚 WORD STUDY
estranged: separated from; made a stranger  ·  avowed: openly declared  ·  hypocrite: a person who shows a virtue he does not have  ·  transgress: to break a law or rule
Word origins: Estranged comes through French from Latin extraneus, “foreign, from outside.” Hypocrite comes from Greek hypokrites, “actor.” Transgress comes from Latin transgredi, “to step across.”
💪 KEY POINT

The forest lets Hester and Dimmesdale say what the town would not hear, and the narrator allows the sunshine. He still weighs the escape against what each owes the truth, and ends the scene with the letter back on Hester’s breast.

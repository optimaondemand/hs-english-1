📖 FOCUS
When does a question persuade better than a statement?
A rhetorical question is asked for its effect, not for an answer. In argument it can expose a contradiction, point to a consequence, or turn a paragraph toward its claim. It works only when the reader’s answer is the one the writer wants, and when the writer supplies the answer soon after.

📚 PART 2 · THE RHETORICAL QUESTION

A good rhetorical question has a single obvious answer and a job to do. Three jobs are common in literary argument: expose a contradiction in a character or institution; point to a consequence of a choice; turn a paragraph from evidence toward the claim.

THREE RULES
1. Ask only questions whose answer the reader will give your way.
2. Follow the question with its answer in the next sentence, or place it so the next sentence supplies one.
3. Never use a question as the thesis. A thesis is a claim, and a claim is a statement.
ON THE BOARD · WEAK, THEN STRONG
Isn’t justice important?
If a court punishes everyone who contradicts its witnesses, what could ever count against them?
Is Proctor a hero? (as a thesis)
Proctor is a hero because he chooses his name over his life.
Why does Hester stay silent? She protects the one man who could have spoken for her.
What did Elizabeth gain by lying? She saved nothing, and she cost her husband his last chance.
Struck-through lines fail: the first has an answer no one needs, the second is a question where a claim belongs. Gold marks the questions that work, with answers following.

💪 THE ONE THING TO CARRY FORWARD

A rhetorical question leads the reader to the writer’s claim. One per paragraph, answered, and never in place of the thesis.

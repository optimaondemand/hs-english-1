LESSON · READING
Act One: How a Witch Hunt Starts
Reading lesson
📖 FOCUS
A sick girl, a forest, and a frightened minister. How does that become a village full of accused neighbors?
Act One shows the mechanism of a witch hunt in a single evening: a secret, a fear, an authority, a name. This lesson traces that sequence and the motives behind it.

📚 PART 2 · EXPOSITION: WHAT THE ACT ESTABLISHES

Exposition is the background a story supplies before its main conflict ignites: the setting, the relationships, and the events that came earlier. Act One is dense with it. The setting is the house of Reverend Parris in Salem, in the spring of 1692. The act also tells the reader about the village’s old quarrels: Parris’s fight with the congregation over his pay, the Putnams’ grievances and their grief over children who died, and the earlier affair between Proctor and Abigail that Proctor has ended.

Miller delivers this background through dialogue and through commentary in the stage directions, so the reader learns who holds grudges before anyone is accused. When the accusations begin, each one lands on a person with a history.

HOW A WITCH HUNT STARTS · FIVE STEPS IN ACT ONE
1
A hidden act is discovered
Parris finds girls from the village in the forest at night, and one of them, Betty, lies unresponsive.
2
Fear of punishment
Abigail denies wrongdoing; Parris fears scandal and the loss of his position.
3
Authority seeks a cause
Reverend Hale arrives with his books to examine the girls.
4
A name is offered
Under questioning, Abigail points to Tituba.
5
Confession is rewarded; naming spreads
Tituba confesses and names others; Abigail and Betty add names of their own.
Each step turns a private fear into a public accusation. Step 5 is the engine: naming others is the one move that protects the person who makes it.

📚 WORD STUDY
exposition: background supplied before the main conflict  ·  motive: the reason a character acts  ·  scapegoat: a person made to bear blame for others  ·  vindictive: eager for revenge
Word origins: Exposition comes from Latin exponere, “to set out.” Motive comes from Latin movere, “to move.” Scapegoat comes from the goat in an ancient ritual that was sent into the wilderness carrying a community’s sins. Vindictive comes from Latin vindicta, “revenge.”

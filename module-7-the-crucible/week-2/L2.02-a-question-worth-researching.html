🎯 FOCUS
A theme is a subject. A research question is something sources can settle.
The cross-text essay follows one theme across The Crucible and at least two earlier course texts, with one researched source. That source has to answer a question, and the question has to be narrow enough to answer and open enough to argue. This lesson covers how to build it and how to test it.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can turn a theme into a question that research can settle.
2
I can choose a historical or critical angle and match it to a type of source.
3
I can apply the four-question credibility check and record an MLA entry as I find a source.

📚 PART 1 · BUILDING THE QUESTION
💭 WARM-UP
Which of these questions about Act Two would send you to a library?

Any of the three needs a source, and each leads to a different angle. A good research question starts as something unresolved in the reading and ends as a sentence a source can answer.

FROM THEME TO QUESTION · FOUR NARROWING STEPS
1 · THEME
justice · loyalty · family · duty
2 · THREE TEXTS
The Crucible plus two or more earlier course texts
3 · ANGLE
Salem, 1692 · the 1950s hearings · criticism of an earlier text
4 · QUESTION
one sentence research can settle
Each step removes options. The question at the bottom is the only one the sources have to answer.

Four tests catch most weak questions.

The questionVerdict
Why did the Salem witch trials happen?Too broad. Whole libraries answer it. A paper could only summarize.
How many people were accused in Salem in 1692?Too small. One fact, with no argument. This is a lookup.
Was Abigail Williams a villain?Not researchable as written. No evidence settles it. Rewrite it as a question about actions or effects.
How do scholars explain the spread of accusations in Salem Village, and where does Miller’s version agree or differ?Answerable. Narrow enough to research and open enough that informed readers disagree.
📚 PART 2 · THREE ANGLES AND THEIR SOURCES

The research question comes from one of three angles. Each calls for a different kind of source.

Salem, 1692
Asks how the historical events compare with the play.
Sources: Scholarly articles and books on the trials; reference entries; primary records held by archives and libraries.
Sample question: How do scholars explain the spread of accusations in Salem Village?
The 1950s hearings
Asks how the period shaped the play and its first audiences.
Sources: Scholarly articles on McCarthyism and the arts; period newspapers; government records of the hearings.
Sample question: How did the congressional investigations of the 1950s shape the way audiences read the play?
Criticism of an earlier text
Asks what critics say about a text the question will compare to the play.
Sources: Scholarly articles on the text; introductions to critical editions.
Sample question: How have critics read the clash between family loyalty and public law in Electra?

Search a database or archive by name and keyword, open each result, and confirm it exists before it goes on a list. Database access for JSTOR is on the Research & JSTOR Access page in Start Here.

WORTH KNOWING · AI AND SOURCES
AI tools can help turn a vague question into search terms. They also produce article titles, authors, and page numbers that look real and do not exist. Every source on a list is one the student opened.
📚 PART 3 · TESTING AND RECORDING SOURCES

Module 5 introduced the four-question credibility check: currency (when it was written), relevance (whether it addresses the question), authority (who wrote it and where it appeared), and purpose (to inform, argue, or sell). Bias by omission applies to all four: a source can be accurate in every sentence and still leave out evidence that cuts the other way. The weight of each question shifts with the angle.

Salem, 1692CurrencyInterpretations of the trials have changed over time. Check the date and what has been argued since.
The 1950s hearingsPurposePopular pages on the period often argue. Identify the angle before borrowing a claim.
Criticism of an earlier textAuthorityCheck the author’s field and the periodical that published the article.

Record the entry when you find it

Copy the author, title, publication, date, and address while the page is open. MLA entries follow a set pattern.

MLA PATTERNS
Scholarly article: Last, First. “Title of Article.” Title of Periodical, vol. #, no. #, Year, pp. #–#. JSTOR, address.
Web page: Author or Organization. “Title of Page.” Title of Site, Publisher, Day Month Year, address.

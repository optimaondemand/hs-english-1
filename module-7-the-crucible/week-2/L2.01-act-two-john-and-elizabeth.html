Act Two: John and Elizabeth Proctor
Lesson
🎯 FOCUS
Act One shows a village. Act Two shows one house.
Miller moves the crisis from Parris’s house into the Proctors’ kitchen. A marriage already under strain meets a court that now reaches into private rooms. The act turns on what each visitor brings in and what John can and cannot say about it.

📚 PART 1 · THE HOUSE AND THE ARRIVALS
💭 WARM-UP
You know something that would stop a harmful rumor, but telling it would expose a mistake of your own. What is the first thing you weigh?

Act Two takes place in the Proctors’ home, eight days after Act One. The public conflict of the first act (the girls, Parris, the accusations) has not left; it has moved indoors. The first scene is a marriage. John and Elizabeth are courteous and guarded. Elizabeth knows about the affair with Abigail, and she presses John on whether he has been alone with her since. Their honesty with each other is partial, and the act measures how much weight that partial honesty can bear.

ACT TWO · SEVEN ARRIVALS, ONE KITCHEN
1
Supper
John and Elizabeth speak with care. Her suspicion about Abigail sits under every sentence.
2
Mary Warren returns
She now serves the court. She reports who will hang and gives Elizabeth a doll she sewed during the proceedings.
3
Hale questions the household
The minister asks about church attendance and has John recite the commandments.
4
Giles and Francis arrive
Both men report that their wives have been arrested.
5
The warrant
Cheever and Herrick come for Elizabeth. A needle is found in the doll.
6
Elizabeth is taken
John tears the warrant. It does not stop the arrest.
7
John turns to Mary
He presses her to testify to what she knows, and she refuses out of fear.
Gold marks the two arrivals that turn private suspicion into official evidence.
📚 PART 2 · THREE PRESSURES

Each visitor in the timeline brings the court a step closer. Three pressures build across the act.

The court enters the house

Mary Warren lives with the Proctors, and she has been sitting in court. She speaks as an official, announces the hangings, and offers the doll as a gift. Her new authority is the first sign that the household no longer controls what happens inside it.

Faith is measured by performance

Hale does not accuse anyone. He tests the household: how often the family attends church, why a son is unbaptized, whether John can recite the commandments. John stumbles over one of them. In this court, a lapse in practice reads as evidence about the soul.

An ordinary object becomes proof

Mary made the doll herself and left a needle in it for safekeeping. Abigail, at dinner in Parris’s house, claimed that Elizabeth’s spirit had driven a needle into her. When Cheever finds the needle in the doll, an innocent object is read through an accusation already made. The audience knows what Abigail wants, which makes the scene an example of dramatic irony: the characters see a crime, and the audience sees a motive.

📚 PART 3 · JOHN’S POSITION

John knows from Abigail’s own words that the girls’ claims began as pretense. He has told Hale and Elizabeth. Telling the court would end the fraud, but it would also force him to explain how he knows, which means admitting that he was alone with Abigail and why. He has no witness and Abigail has the court’s trust. Elizabeth sees the danger and wants him to act. The act leaves the choice open and puts Elizabeth’s freedom on the other side of it.

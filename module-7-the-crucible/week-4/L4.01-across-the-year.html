📚 PART 1 · THE FOUR THREADS

A thread is a question a text keeps asking and answers in its own way. A topic only says what a text contains. The cross-text essay follows one thread (justice, loyalty, family, or duty) through The Crucible and at least two earlier texts.

Read a row left to right for one question answered many ways. Read a column top to bottom for the range of one text. Each cell is a starting point, not a full list.

Reading across a row

Take loyalty. Gawain keeps the girdle back and breaks the exchange-of-winnings pledge to save his life. Hester refuses to name Dimmesdale and carries the cost in public. Elizabeth Proctor lies once, for her husband, and the lie helps destroy him. Each protects a person; each pays in truth, and each text judges the payment differently.

Reading down a column

The Crucible touches all four threads: the court is justice, the Proctor household is family, Elizabeth and Giles Corey are loyalty, and Hale is duty against conscience. A text that reaches several threads gives more than one way into an argument.

What makes a workable trio

▶
All three texts engage the same thread.
▶
At least two answer it differently.
▶
One of the three can be tested by the researched source.

📚 WORD STUDY
thread: a recurring question that runs through several texts.  ·  conscience: a person’s inner sense of right and wrong.  ·  loyalty: faithfulness to a person or cause.  ·  duty: what a role or promise requires.  ·  justice: giving each person what is due.
Word origins: thread comes from Old English thræd, related to thráwan, “to twist.” Conscience comes from Latin conscientia, “knowing with” oneself. Loyal comes through Old French loial from Latin legalis, “of the law.” Duty comes through Anglo-French dueté from due, what is owed. Justice comes from Latin iustitia, from ius, “right, law.”

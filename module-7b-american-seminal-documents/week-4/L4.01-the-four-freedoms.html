Holding a Nation to Its Words
MODULE QUESTION
What happens to a promise a nation makes to itself?

The Four Freedoms: Enlarging What a Right Means
Lesson
📖 FOCUS
Two of Roosevelt's four freedoms would have been familiar to anyone who had read the Bill of Rights. The other two were something new.
January 1941. The United States is not yet in the war. In his annual message to Congress, Roosevelt names four freedoms he says should exist “everywhere in the world.” The passage uses a familiar form to introduce an unfamiliar idea.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can explain how a speaker uses a familiar structure to carry a new claim.
2
I can distinguish a freedom stated as a limit on government from a freedom stated as a condition to be provided.
3
I can identify what a speaker gains and risks by widening the scope of a claim.

📚 PART 1 · THE FAMILIAR SHAPE

Roosevelt builds the passage as a list of four, each item opening the same way. You named that construction in Week 1 and used it in Week 3. It is the oldest trick in public prose, and it works here for a particular reason: a list that sounds traditional can carry items that are not.

THE FOUR FREEDOMS · 6 JANUARY 1941
“The first is freedom of speech and expression — everywhere in the world.

The second is freedom of every person to worship God in his own way — everywhere in the world.

The third is freedom from want — which, translated into world terms, means economic understandings which will secure to every nation a healthy peacetime life for its inhabitants — everywhere in the world.

The fourth is freedom from fear — which, translated into world terms, means a world-wide reduction of armaments to such a point and in such a thorough fashion that no nation will be in a position to commit an act of physical aggression against any neighbor — anywhere in the world.”
Four items, one shape, one phrase repeated at the end of each. Two of the four are printed in a different color. Look at them before reading on and see whether you can say why.

📚 PART 2 · FREEDOM OF, AND FREEDOM FROM
TWO KINDS OF FREEDOM IN ONE LIST
FREEDOMS 1 AND 2 · “FREEDOM OF”
Speech. Worship.
These are already in the First Amendment, and they work the way you saw in Week 2: as things the government is forbidden to interfere with. To deliver them, a government mainly has to refrain.
FREEDOMS 3 AND 4 · “FREEDOM FROM”
Want. Fear.
These are not in the Bill of Rights, and they cannot be delivered by refraining. Nobody becomes free from hunger because a government leaves them alone. To deliver these, a government has to act.
The list looks uniform. Halfway through, the kind of thing being promised quietly changes.

What the form is doing

This is the move worth taking away from the lesson. By giving all four items the same grammatical shape and the same closing phrase, Roosevelt makes the third and fourth sound like members of a family the audience already accepts. The parallel structure argues that these belong together. It does not prove it.

Notice, too, that he defines the last two on the spot — “which, translated into world terms, means…” — and does not define the first two at all. That is a tell. A speaker defines what the audience might not accept and leaves alone what they already grant. You saw Madison do the same thing with faction, for the same reason.

Everywhere in the world

Then there is the phrase at the end of every item. American presidents in 1941 did not usually claim jurisdiction over what happened in other countries' politics. Repeating everywhere in the world four times converts a domestic list into a statement about what all people are owed, which is a very large claim for a speech about a defense budget.

What it gains is enormous scope. What it risks is equally clear: a promise made everywhere is a promise that can be tested anywhere, including at home. That is precisely what happens in the lesson that follows this one.

✎ CHECK YOURSELF · PICK THE BEST ANSWER
1. What is the real difference between the first two freedoms and the last two?
2. Why does Roosevelt define the third and fourth freedoms but not the first and second?
3. What is the risk of ending each item with “everywhere in the world”?
📚 WORD STUDY
want (this sense) — lack of necessities; poverty.  ·  armaments — military weapons and equipment.  ·  aggression — an attack begun without provocation.  ·  secure (verb) — to make certain of; to guarantee.  ·  provision — something supplied to meet a need.
Word origins: want is another word to run the three-step check on — here it means lack, not desire, which is its older sense and still survives in phrases like for want of a nail. Notice that secure is the same verb the Declaration used in 1776: governments are instituted “to secure these rights.” Roosevelt is reaching for that word on purpose. Copia: write a fifth freedom in Roosevelt's exact form, including the closing phrase. Then write one sentence saying whether yours needs a government to refrain or to act.
📓 PRACTICE

Write:

▶
Which of the four freedoms would be hardest to prove a country had achieved? Name what evidence would count.
▶
Roosevelt reuses the Declaration's verb secure. Write one sentence on what he gains by borrowing it.
▶
One sentence: is a promise made “everywhere in the world” stronger or weaker than one made to a single nation? Defend your answer.
💪 KEY IDEA

Parallel structure can make new claims sound like old ones. That is worth knowing from both sides of the page: it is a genuine tool when you are writing, and something to watch for when you are reading.

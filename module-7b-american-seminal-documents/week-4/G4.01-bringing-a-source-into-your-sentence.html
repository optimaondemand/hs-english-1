📖 FOCUS
A quotation dropped into a paragraph on its own is the most common flaw in research writing. Here is how to fix it permanently.
Module 1 cited a play, Module 2 an anonymous poem, and Module 5 a short story. The paper also needs speeches, government documents, and scholarly articles. The principle does not change; the entries do.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can integrate a quotation into a sentence of your own three different ways.
2
I can cite a speech, a government document, and a scholarly article correctly in text.
3
I can build a Works Cited entry for each of those source types.
📚 PART 1 · NEVER LET A QUOTATION STAND ALONE
THE DROPPED QUOTATION, AND THREE REPAIRS
Dropped: Lincoln wanted the country to heal. “With malice toward none; with charity for all.”
Two sentences with no grammatical relationship. The reader has to guess how they connect.

1. Signal phrase: Lincoln closes by asking for a peace conducted “with malice toward none; with charity for all.”

2. Colon: Lincoln names the terms of the peace he wants: “with malice toward none; with charity for all.”
The colon works only when what comes before it is already a complete sentence.

3. Blended: By asking for “charity for all” rather than a settlement of accounts, Lincoln rules out the punitive peace his audience expected.
The strongest of the three. The quoted words are load-bearing inside your own sentence, and you quote only what you need.

Use the third form most. Quote the two or three words that carry the meaning, and write the rest yourself. Long quotations are usually a sign that the writer has not decided what matters in them yet.

📚 PART 2 · THREE SOURCE TYPES

The rule behind every in-text citation is the same: point the reader to the first thing in your Works Cited entry, and tell them where in the source to look. What changes is what that first thing is, and what “where” means when a source has no pages.

Source typeIn your sentenceWorks Cited entry
A speech with no page numbersName the speaker in your sentence and no parenthetical is needed: Lincoln closes his second inaugural by asking…Lincoln, Abraham. “Second Inaugural Address.” 4 Mar. 1865. Avalon Project, Yale Law School, avalon.law.yale.edu/19th_century/lincoln2.asp.
A government document with no authorUse a shortened title in place of an author: (“Declaration”) — the same rule you used for the anonymous Sir Gawain.“Declaration of Independence: A Transcription.” National Archives, 4 July 1776, www.archives.gov/founding-docs/declaration-transcript.
A scholarly article from JSTORAuthor and page: (Maier 47). This is the ordinary case and the one you already know.Maier, Pauline. “The Strange History of the Declaration of Independence.” Journal of American History, vol. 84, no. 2, 1997, pp. 43–62. JSTOR, www.jstor.org/stable/xxxxxxx.
📌 THE ONE RULE UNDER ALL OF THEM
Whatever begins the Works Cited entry must be what appears in your in-text citation. Author if there is one; title if there is not. If a reader cannot get from your parenthesis to the right line of your list in one step, the citation is not doing its job — and that, rather than the punctuation, is what citation is actually for.

✍️ TRY IT
Take one quotation from each of your four bibliography sources. Integrate each one into a sentence of your own using the blended form, with a correct in-text citation. Then write the four Works Cited entries.
⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · CITATION GENERATORS AND AI
Citation tools and AI assistants both produce entries that are formatted convincingly and are often wrong — a missing volume number, an invented page range, a publisher that never existed. Check every entry against the source itself. A citation is a promise that a reader can find what you found, and that promise is yours, not the tool's.
💪 KEY IDEA

A quotation is not evidence until you have said what it shows. Put the borrowed words inside a sentence of your own, and the reader always knows why they are reading them.

Lesson
📖 FOCUS
March 1865. The war is nearly won. The winning side expects a victory speech. It does not get one.
The task is to evaluate a speaker's reasoning: not whether the conclusion is agreeable, but whether the argument holds. The speech suits that task because its reasoning is unusual and its audience did not entirely like it.

2
I can explain what a speaker gains and gives up by declining an expected move.
3
I can distinguish between naming a cause and assigning blame.

📚 PART 1 · THE SPEECH NOBODY ASKED FOR

By March 1865 the outcome was no longer in doubt. Lincoln had been reelected. Sherman had marched through Georgia. Richmond would fall within weeks. An inaugural address at that moment had an obvious shape available to it: name the victory, honor the winners, condemn the losers, promise a hard peace.

Lincoln declines every part of that. He gives no war news — “little that is new could be presented.” He does not predict victory. And when he reaches the question of who caused the war, he does something his listeners did not expect.

📚 PART 2 · NAMING A CAUSE WITHOUT AWARDING INNOCENCE
THREE MOVES, IN ORDER
MOVE 1 · HE NAMES THE CAUSE PLAINLY
“These slaves constituted a peculiar and powerful interest. All knew that this interest was, somehow, the cause of the war.”
No hedging. He does not say the war was about states' rights or tariffs or misunderstanding. He names slavery, and he says everyone already knew.

MOVE 2 · HE REFUSES TO MAKE ONE SIDE THE VILLAIN
“Both read the same Bible, and pray to the same God; and each invokes His aid against the other… but let us judge not, that we be not judged.”
He treats the war as an offense the whole country is answerable for, North included — not as a crime committed by the South against the innocent.

MOVE 3 · HE TURNS THAT INTO A POLICY
“With malice toward none; with charity for all… let us strive on to finish the work we are in; to bind up the nation's wounds.”
The conclusion is not a feeling. It is an instruction about how the war should end and what should happen afterward.

Why Move 2 is the hard one

Notice carefully what Lincoln is and is not doing, because it is easy to get wrong in both directions.

He is not saying both sides were equally right, and he is not softening what slavery was. Elsewhere in the same speech he calls it an offense, and he says that if the war continues “until every drop of blood drawn with the lash shall be paid by another drawn with the sword,” that judgment would still be just. That is not a neutral sentence.

What he is doing is refusing to let the victorious side treat itself as innocent. The North had profited from slavery for generations and had written protections for it into the Constitution. So he distributes responsibility for the offense while keeping the offense itself named and condemned. That combination — a clear judgment about the wrong, a shared judgment about the wrongdoers — is what makes the speech unusual, and it is what makes the closing instruction possible. You cannot ask for a peace without malice if you have spent the speech proving the other side is uniquely wicked.

Evaluating it

Your job as a reader is not to decide whether you like this. It is to ask whether the reasoning works, and reasonable people have answered differently.

A strength you can defendA question you can press
The argument is consistent: he names one cause, applies one standard to both sides, and derives his policy from that standard rather than from sentiment.Does shared responsibility for slavery mean shared responsibility for the war? Those are different claims, and the speech moves between them quickly.
He gives evidence for the shared-guilt claim rather than asserting it — the North's long participation in and profit from slavery is a matter of record.Frederick Douglass, who was present, admired the speech, and also spent years arguing that reconciliation between white Americans North and South tended to come at the expense of Black Americans. That is a serious objection to the policy, not to the logic.
The conclusion is actionable. “Bind up the nation's wounds” names a thing a government can actually do.Does “malice toward none” leave any room for accountability? A reader can accept the reasoning and still ask what justice for the freed people required.
⚖️ HOW TO ARGUE WITH A SPEECH LIKE THIS
Notice the form of every question in the right-hand column. None of them is “Lincoln was wrong” or “this speech is bad.” Each one identifies a specific joint in the argument and asks whether it holds. That is what evaluating a speaker's reasoning means, and it is a great deal more useful — and more difficult — than deciding whether you are on the speaker's side.

2. Why does declining to give a victory speech matter to the argument?

📚 WORD STUDY
malice — the desire to do harm.  ·  charity (older sense) — goodwill toward others, not donation.  ·  invoke — to call on for help or authority.  ·  scourge — a whip, and by extension a severe punishment.  ·  rend — to tear apart violently.
Word origins: charity comes through Latin caritas, meaning costly love or regard — another word whose modern sense has narrowed, so run the three-step check from the Preamble lesson on it. Malice is from malus, “bad,” the same root as malady and malfunction. Copia: rewrite “with malice toward none; with charity for all” in plain modern English. Then write one sentence on why the original is harder to forget.

💪 KEY IDEA

The hardest thing a speaker can do is tell their own side something it does not want to hear. When you find a writer doing that, slow down: you are usually looking at the part of the argument they cared most about.

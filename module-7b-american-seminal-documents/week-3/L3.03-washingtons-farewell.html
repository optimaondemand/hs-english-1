LITERATURE
Washington's Farewell: Advice, and the Reasons Under It
Lesson
📖 FOCUS
Anyone can give advice. The question worth asking is whether the reasons given actually hold the advice up.
Washington's Farewell Address is a long letter of warnings from a man leaving power voluntarily. Each warning separates into what he advises and why he advises it; the test is whether the second supports the first.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can separate a writer's advice from the reasoning offered for it.
2
I can judge whether a stated reason actually supports the recommendation it is attached to.
3
I can explain how an author builds a series of connected ideas across a long text.
📋 MATERIALS
▶
Washington's Farewell Address, 1796 — long; read the excerpt sections named below

📚 PART 1 · THE SITUATION

In 1796 Washington could almost certainly have stayed President for as long as he lived. He chose not to run again, and published a letter explaining why he was leaving and what he thought the country should watch out for. It was printed in newspapers rather than delivered as a speech; most Americans read it rather than heard it.

The letter is long. Read the sections on union, on party spirit, and on foreign relations. You do not need the whole thing for this lesson.

📚 PART 2 · SPLITTING ADVICE FROM REASON

Reading advice is easy. Reading the reasons under advice is the skill. Here are three of his warnings, with the reason he gives, separated:

He advisesBecause — his stated reasonDoes the reason hold the advice up?
Guard the union between the states above almost everything else.Separate regions would end up as rival powers, needing armies against each other, and standing armies threaten liberty at home.Strong. The reason connects directly to a consequence he has already established the reader cares about.
Beware the spirit of party.Parties organized by region or faction turn government into a contest for revenge, and eventually invite a strongman who promises to end the chaos.Strong, with a gap. He describes the danger vividly but says little about how a free country is supposed to operate without organized parties. The advice outruns the reason.
Avoid permanent alliances with foreign nations.A permanent alliance drags you into quarrels that are not yours, and attachment to a foreign nation lets a small group inside your country serve that nation's interest instead of your own.Strong for its moment. But notice the reason is about a young, weak, distant country. A reader applying the advice to a different situation would need to check whether the reason still applies.

What the third row teaches

This is the most useful habit in the lesson. A piece of advice travels easily; the reason under it often does not. Washington's argument against permanent alliances rests on specific facts about the United States in 1796 — small, militarily weak, an ocean away from Europe's wars. Someone quoting the advice two centuries later is borrowing the sentence without the reason that made it good.

That is not a criticism of Washington. He was writing about his own moment, and said so. It is a warning about how the letter gets used. When you find a piece of advice being quoted, always ask: is the reason still true?

How the letter is built

Notice also the order. He does not lead with the warnings. He begins by explaining why he is stepping down, then establishes the value of the union, and only then names the things that threaten it. Party spirit and foreign entanglement are presented as threats to something the reader has already been persuaded to care about. Reverse the order and the warnings sound like the complaints of a tired man; in this order they sound like the defense of something valuable.

You have now seen that structure three times: the Declaration, Federalist No. 10, and this. Establish what the reader values, then show what endangers it. It is available to you in your own paper.

📚 WORD STUDY
faction — carried over from Federalist No. 10; note Washington uses it the same way.  ·  solicitude — anxious care for someone's wellbeing.  ·  baneful — causing ruin.  ·  entangle — to catch and hold in something difficult to escape.  ·  vicissitude — a change of circumstances, usually unwelcome.
Word origins: baneful comes from Old English bana, “killer” — a much heavier word than it now sounds. Entangle is worth noticing as a metaphor rather than a term: Washington chose a word from nets and thickets, and the image is doing part of the arguing. Copia: take one warning from the letter and write it twice — once as Washington's advice with his reason attached, and once as the advice alone. Then one sentence on how the second version could be misused.
⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · ADVICE WITHOUT ITS REASONS
Ask an AI tool what Washington warned against and you will get a tidy list: parties, sectionalism, foreign entanglements. Notice that the list is exactly the part that travels badly. The reasons — which are what let you judge whether the advice applies to any situation you actually face — are what gets dropped in summarizing. That is a good reason to read the letter itself, and a good reason to be careful about anything you know only as a list.
📓 PRACTICE

💪 KEY IDEA

Advice is portable and reasons are not. Whenever someone hands you a rule with a famous name attached, ask what the rule was originally a response to. Half the time the answer changes what you do with it.

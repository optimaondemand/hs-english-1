Parallel Structure in Public Writing
Grammar & Style
📖 FOCUS
Why does “He has refused… He has forbidden… He has dissolved…” hit harder than the same facts written three different ways?
Parallel structure appeared in Module 1, inside a Greek tragedy. Here it does a different job: holding a public document together and making a list feel like a case.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can keep items in a series in the same grammatical form.
2
I can repeat an opening phrase deliberately to build force across sentences.
3
I can recognize when the repetition should stop.
📚 PART 1 · THE SAME SHAPE, ON PURPOSE

Parallel structure means matching forms: if the first item in a series is a verb phrase, every item is a verb phrase. Break the pattern and a reader trips, even if they cannot say why.

BROKEN, THEN FIXED
Broken: The King dissolved the legislature, was refusing to approve laws, and the courts were obstructed by him.
Three items, three different shapes: past tense, past progressive, then passive. The sentence wobbles.

Fixed: The King dissolved the legislature, refused to approve laws, and obstructed the courts.
Three past-tense verbs, three objects. Now the reader can feel the list as a list.

Repetition across whole sentences

The Declaration goes further than matching items inside one sentence. It repeats the opening of sentence after sentence: He has. He has. He has. Starting successive sentences with the same words is a deliberate device, and it does something a varied opening cannot: it removes the writer from view. Every sentence has the same subject and the same shape, so the only thing changing is the offense. The form itself says this keeps happening.

It is why a coach saying “we showed up, we worked, we won” lands harder than “we showed up, our work was good, and a victory resulted.”

📚 PART 2 · WHEN TO STOP

Repetition is a tool with a cost. Used for twenty-seven charges, it builds. Used for three sentences in a five-paragraph essay, it sounds like a chant, and a reader starts noticing the technique instead of the argument. Two rules keep you safe:

RuleWhat it looks like in your own writing
Match the forms in any series. Always.If your thesis lists three things a character wants, all three should be the same kind of phrase.
Repeat an opening only when the repetition is the point.Use it when your claim is that something happened again and again. If your claim is not about a pattern, vary your openings instead.
✎ CHECK YOURSELF · PICK THE BEST ANSWER
1. Which sentence keeps its series parallel?
2. In which case is repeating “She has…” at the start of four sentences the right choice?
✍️ TRY IT
Write three sentences about the Declaration that all begin with the same two words, making a single point about a pattern in the document. Then write one more sentence that deliberately breaks the pattern to land your conclusion. That last break is where a reader wakes up — which is exactly why the Declaration's final paragraph stops saying He has and starts saying We, therefore.
💪 KEY IDEA

Matching forms is a rule you follow every time. Repeating an opening is a decision you make when the sameness is the message. Knowing which is which is the difference between writing that sounds controlled and writing that sounds mechanical.

📖 FOCUS
Twenty-seven charges against a king. Are they proof, or are they just a long list?
Evidence is tested with three questions: is it relevant, is there enough of it, and is it fair. The Declaration's list of grievances is the most famous list of complaints in the English language.

1
I can test a piece of evidence for relevance, sufficiency, and fairness.
2
I can explain why the grievances are written as a list of the King's actions rather than the colonists' feelings.
3
I can describe what the document leaves out, and say why a reader should notice.

📚 PART 1 · WHY THEY ALL START THE SAME WAY
💭 WARM-UP
Read these two sentences. Which one is harder for an opponent to argue with?

A list of actions, not a list of feelings

Look at how the grievances are built. Almost every one begins the same way: He has. He has refused. He has forbidden. He has dissolved. He has obstructed. He has plundered. The subject is always the King, the verb is always something he did, and the sentence is always short enough to check.

This is a deliberate choice, and it is worth understanding because you can use it. A reader who does not already agree with you cannot be argued out of a feeling, because there is nothing in a feeling to disagree with. But a reader can be shown an action. Once the action is on the table, the argument moves to ground where evidence decides things.

Notice also what the repetition does to a reader who reads all twenty-seven in a row. No single grievance would justify a revolution. A king dissolving one legislature is a bad month, not a tyranny. The document is counting on accumulation: the case is not any one charge but the pattern all of them make. That is the argument the form itself is making, before you read a word of the content.

📚 PART 2 · PUTTING THE EVIDENCE ON TRIAL
THREE QUESTIONS YOU ALREADY KNOW · NOW APPLIED TO A REAL CHARGE
THE CHARGE
“For quartering large bodies of armed troops among us: For protecting them, by a mock Trial, from punishment for any Murders which they should commit on the Inhabitants of these States.”
IS IT RELEVANT?
The rule said a government must secure life and liberty. Soldiers who kill without consequence go straight at both. Yes — and directly.
IS THERE ENOUGH?
One charge would not carry a revolution. Twenty-seven, spanning taxes, courts, trade, and troops, is a pattern. The number is doing work.
IS IT FAIR?
This is the hard one. The list gives the King's actions and no reasons for them, and it names no colonial provocation. Hold this thought.
Two questions the document answers well. One it does not answer at all.

What a one-sided case looks like

The Declaration is a legal brief, not a history. Its job is to make the strongest possible case for one side, and it does not pretend otherwise. Every charge is written from the colonists' point of view; not one of them offers the King's reason for acting. Parliament, for instance, argued that the taxes the colonists objected to were paying down debt from a war fought partly to defend those same colonies. That argument appears nowhere in the document, because the document is not trying to be balanced.

Noticing this is not an accusation. A defense attorney's closing statement is one-sided too, and nobody thinks it is dishonest for being so. But a careful reader knows what kind of text they are holding. A brief tells you what one side wants you to conclude. If you want to know what happened, you need the other brief as well — which is exactly what your research this module will give you.

⚖️ ONE MORE OMISSION, AND A LARGE ONE
The principle in Part 2 says all men are created equal. Many of the men who signed beneath that sentence held other people in slavery, and the document says nothing about it. Jefferson's draft did contain a passage attacking the slave trade; the Congress struck it out before adopting the text. This is not a modern complaint imported backward: it was noticed at the time, and it is the gap that Lincoln reaches for at Gettysburg in Week 3 and that King reaches for in Week 4. Keep it in view. The whole module is built on the question of what happens to a promise a nation makes and does not keep.

📚 WORD STUDY
grievance — a formal statement of a wrong suffered.  ·  brief — a written argument for one side of a case.  ·  accumulation — the force that comes from many items gathered together.  ·  quartering — housing soldiers in private homes by order.  ·  abdicate — to give up a responsibility or a throne.
Word origins: grievance comes from Latin gravis, “heavy” — the same root as grave and gravity; a grievance is a weight you are carrying. Abdicate is ab- (“away”) plus dicare (“to proclaim”), so it literally means to announce yourself out of a duty. Copia: take one grievance from the document and rewrite it twice — once as the King's ministers might have described the same event, and once as a neutral historian would.
⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI AND ONE-SIDED SOURCES
Ask an AI tool about a contested historical event and it will usually give you a smooth, balanced-sounding paragraph. That smoothness can hide the fact that it is summarizing whichever sources were most common in its training, not weighing them. It cannot tell you what a document leaves out, because it has not read the document sitting in front of you. That comparison is the reader's job.
📓 PRACTICE

Put one grievance on trial. Choose any charge from the list, copy it out exactly, and answer:

▶
Relevant? Which part of the self-evident-truths rule does this charge connect to?
▶
Enough? Could this charge stand on its own, or does it need the others around it?
▶
Fair? What would you need to know to judge it — and where might you find that out?
💪 KEY IDEA

A brief is not a lie, but it is not the whole story either. Telling a text that is arguing from a text that is explaining is a reading skill used every day.

📖 FOCUS
How do you tell a reader what a word means without stopping to write a dictionary entry?
Madison needed his reader to accept a precise meaning of faction before the argument could start, and he did it inside a sentence.
🎯 OBJECTIVES

I can define a term with an appositive, without breaking the flow of your sentence.
2

3

📚 PART 1 · THE APPOSITIVE

An appositive is a noun phrase set beside another noun to rename or explain it. Appositives appeared in Module 2 for character detail. Here they carry definitions.

THE SAME INFORMATION, THREE WAYS
Clumsy: Madison writes about factions. A faction is a group united by a shared interest that is adverse to the rights of others. He argues that factions cannot be eliminated.
Three sentences, and the definition sits in a lump the reader has to carry.

Appositive: Madison argues that a faction, a group united by an interest adverse to the rights of others, cannot be eliminated without destroying liberty.
One sentence. The definition rides along inside the commas and the argument keeps moving.

Relative clause: Madison argues that a faction, which he defines as any group whose aims run against the rights of others, may well be a majority.
Use this when you want to credit the definition to its author rather than assert it yourself. In a research paper that difference matters.

The punctuation rule that actually matters

Both constructions take commas when the information is extra — when the sentence would still identify its subject without them. Drop the commas and you change the meaning: the essay that Madison wrote singles out one essay from several, while the essay, which Madison wrote, tells you about an essay already identified. Pairs of commas: never one. An appositive opened with a comma and closed without one is among the most common errors in student papers.

📚 PART 2 · USING IT IN A RESEARCH PAPER

This construction earns its keep when you introduce a source. Compare:

VersionEffect on the reader
Gordon Wood wrote about this. He is a historian of the American Revolution. He argues that…The reader waits three sentences for the point, and the credential feels bolted on.
Gordon Wood, a historian of the American Revolution, argues that…The credential arrives exactly where it is useful — while the reader is deciding whether to trust the claim that follows.

That is the ordinary way scholarly writing establishes authority: not by announcing it in its own sentence, but by tucking it in beside the name. The construction is used in Feeder 2 and in every paragraph of the final paper where a new source appears.

✍️ TRY IT

💪 KEY IDEA

A definition tucked inside a sentence does two jobs at once: it tells the reader what you mean, and it shows you knew the word needed defining. Both of those make a reader trust you.
